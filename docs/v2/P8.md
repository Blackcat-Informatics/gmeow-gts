# Sealing (registry version `2`)

## 0. Scope and notation

This part defines registry version `2` of the format: authenticated encryption of frames with key commitment. It defines the
`keys` and `sealed` frame types, the sealed inner record and its inner types, the sealing cipher `aes256_gcm_siv` with its key
schedule, synthetic nonce and commitment, the one padding policy, the per-key limits, the one key-wrap suite, sealed packs and the
sealed member index, the registry values of version `2`, and the results that state what the construction guarantees, with their
proofs.

The part is written to be checked alone. The container format into which it fits is not part of this document; section 1 states,
as three numbered assumptions, everything the part takes from it. Section 2 defines every primitive with its standard and the
security notion assumed of it; sections 4 to 10 are the construction as exact procedures; sections 11 to 14 are the security
definitions, the theorems, the lemma and the corollaries, with their proofs; section 19 is the closed list of what is assumed and
not proved, and the questions put to the reviewer.

What is proved, and from what. Theorem C (commitment) is proved from the collision resistance of SHA-256 alone. Theorem E
(multi-key deterministic authenticated encryption) is proved in the ideal-cipher model for AES-256 from the assumptions on the key
schedule, on HMAC and on AES-256-GCM-SIV. Lemma K reduces the security of the key wrap to assumptions on X-Wing, HMAC, SHA-256 and
AES-256-GCM. Corollary W extends Theorem E to keys that a file also wraps to recipients. Corollary F carries Theorems E and W to a
reader of a file that holds the writer's segment key directly and holds no recipient key for an entry whose target is a key entry
of the writer: it is the only proof that uses the assumptions about the container. It uses `A-1` (the writer's frame and the
reader's view are computable from public values) and `A-3` (the reader's view is a function of the file); it cites `A-2` only to
record that nothing outside the inputs of `Open` matters; and collision resistance of `H_ctx` is needed only to read "equal `h0`"
as "equal header". The statements of 6.2 (what the associated data binds) and 12.4 (what commitment gives a reader) are read off
from Corollary F and Theorem C. The list of 10.2 (what a keyless party sees) is a description of the clear fields of the records
of this part, not a theorem.

Notation.

- `E(x)` is the deterministic encoding of the value `x` in CBOR (RFC 8949, "Concise Binary Object Representation (CBOR)",
  December 2020, section 4.2.1, "Core Deterministic Encoding Requirements"): every head in its shortest form, every length
  definite.
- The typed notation: `uint` (a CBOR unsigned integer), `bool`, `bytes`, `text`, `digest` (32 bytes in registry versions `1` and
  `2`), `list(T)` (a CBOR array of values of `T`). `enum(E)` is a value of the enumeration `E`, encoded as the `uint` of its code.
  `option(T)` is the variant `[1]` for absent and `[2, x]` for a present value `x` of `T`. A RECORD is a CBOR array of its fields
  in declared order; a VARIANT is a CBOR array whose first element is a tag of its tag enumeration. Every field is always present.
- `bytes32` is written for a `bytes` field whose length MUST be exactly 32; any other length is `malformed_value` with reason
  `wrong_length`.
- `a || b` is the concatenation of byte strings; `len(x)` the length of `x` in bytes; `x[i..j]` the bytes of `x` from index `i`
  included to `j` excluded, from `0`; `0^n` the string of `n` zero bytes.
- `H_ctx(context, message)` is the container's one hash construction (assumption `A-1`): the key-derivation mode of BLAKE3 (J.
  O'Connor, J.-P. Aumasson, S. Neves and Z. Wilcox-O'Hearn, "BLAKE3: one function, fast everywhere", 2020) with the spelling of a
  row of the hash context table as context string and `message` as key material, 32 bytes of output. The rows this document uses
  are `header` (spelling `gts2 header`), `frame` (`gts2 frame`), `payload` (`gts2 payload`), all three of the container (`A-1`),
  and `recipient_key_id` (`gts2 recipient key id`, 3.3); a spelling is hashed as its ASCII bytes, whole, with no length prefix or
  terminator. Every identifier, payload digest and header identifier of section 16 is computed with these spellings.
- `HMAC(K, M)` is HMAC with SHA-256; `HKDF-Extract` and `HKDF-Expand` are those of RFC 5869 with SHA-256 (section 2).
- A frame is TRANSPARENT exactly when its envelope field `transparent` is `true`; every other frame is ORDINARY. A `keys` frame
  and a sealed frame are not transparent (`P8-6`). The ORDINAL of a frame is the number of ordinary frames of its segment before
  it that have an effective identifier (`A-1`); a frame with no effective identifier has no ordinal and is not counted.
- Outcomes of reading. The container reports what a reader finds as FINDINGS, each with a code from the finding table, a fixed
  severity and, for some codes, a reason; a frame whose content the reader does not fold is OPAQUE, with one opaque reason; a
  frame's PAYLOAD STATE says whether its stored bytes matched its payload digest. Section 3 lists the codes this part adds. These
  outcomes matter to sections 3 to 9 and to the vectors; no proof depends on them.
- Requirements are numbered `P8-1`, `P8-2` and on, densely. MUST, MUST NOT and MAY have their standard meaning.

## 1. What this part needs from the container

The three assumptions below are guarantees of the container format, restated so that a proof can use them and a reader can check
them without that format. Corollary F (14.3), for a reader that holds the writer's segment key directly and holds no recipient key
for an entry whose target is a key entry of the writer, uses `A-1` and `A-3`, and cites `A-2` only to record that nothing outside
the inputs of `Open` matters; section 10 uses `A-3`. Theorems C and E, Lemma K and Corollary W are about the abstract scheme of
11.1 and use none of them.

**Assumption `A-1` (what an identifier covers).** A file is a sequence of segments; a segment is an opener, a header or a
resync item (`P1`, "The resync item"), followed by items. A
header is the record `[item_kind, generation, registry_version, hash_algorithm, profile, canonical, lineage]`; `lineage` is absent
in the first segment of a file and otherwise names the HEAD of the previous segment and an `ordinal` (`P1`, "The
header"). A header holds NO
per-file unique or random value: the first segments of two files written with the same settings have equal headers. The segment's
HEADER IDENTIFIER `h0` is the identifier of its opener: `H_ctx(header, E(header))` for a header, and `H_ctx(header,
resync_body)` for a resync item
(`P1`, "The resync item"). A frame is a record of eleven fields: `item_kind`, `frame_type`, `critical`,
`transparent`, `content`, `decoded_length`, `payload_digest`, `storage`, `payload`, `identifier`, `signatures`. Its identifier is
`H_ctx(frame, E(identity))`, where `identity = [predecessor, frame_type, critical, transparent, content, decoded_length,
payload_digest]` and `predecessor` is the effective identifier of the nearest preceding ordinary frame that has one, or `h0` when
there is none. A frame is TRANSPARENT exactly when its envelope field `transparent` is `true`; every other frame is ORDINARY. The
EFFECTIVE IDENTIFIER of an ordinary frame is its stored identifier, or the identifier computed for it when its link state is
`healed`, that is, when the stored identifier is damaged and the item directly after it, a frame whose link explains the
damage, witnesses it (`P1`,
"Healing"). A frame whose `identifier`
field is not a `bytes` item of the digest length has NO stored identifier: if elements 1 to 6 are well typed its effective
identifier is the COMPUTED one, and if they are not it has none. A frame with no effective identifier is an item of its segment
with a position; it is not an ordinary frame for any count: it has no ordinal, is not a leaf of the range, is not counted by a
commit or an index, is not a predecessor and is never the head. The HEAD of a segment is the effective identifier of its last
ordinary frame that has one, or the header identifier when there is none. So the identifier is a function of the frame's type,
flags, content record, decoded length and payload digest, and of every earlier ordinary frame of the segment that has an effective
identifier and of the header; it is a function of nothing else. `payload_digest` is `H_ctx(payload, d)` for the frame's decoded
payload `d`, and `decoded_length` is `len(d)`; this part defines both for a sealed frame over its ciphertext (section 6). The
spellings of the three container rows used here are `gts2 header` (for `h0`), `gts2 frame` (for an identifier) and `gts2 payload`
(for a payload digest). What this part needs of `A-1`: that these preimages are exactly as stated, so that a party without keys
computes every identifier and digest (Corollary F and the known answers of section 16), and, only to read "equal `h0`" as "equal
header", that `H_ctx` has no known collision.

**Assumption `A-2` (what lies outside the identifier).** The fields `storage`, `payload` and `signatures` of a frame are not
inputs of any identifier. A party that rewrites them changes no identifier; the stored bytes are bound to the identifier only
through the check `payload_digest = H_ctx(payload, decoded payload)`, which any party can compute. A signature is a separate
record over an identifier; it is never an input of the seal. Neither the seal nor the identifier binds storage or signatures.

**Assumption `A-3` (what a party without keys sees and can do, and what a reader computes).** Every field of every item is
readable by every party. A party that holds no key can delimit every item, compute and check every identifier and every payload
digest, and count frames; it can also recompute every identifier after changing any field, since no identifier involves a secret.
So an adversary may replace, insert, delete, reorder or rewrite any item and re-link the chain, and the chain alone never
authenticates (an authenticating signature, outside this document, does). A reader's view of a segment (its items in order, the
fields of each, the ordinal of each frame and the segment's `h0`) is a deterministic function of the bytes of the file alone.

What follows from the three. The seal must bind, in its own associated data, every clear value that a key holder relies on (the
header through `h0`, the key and the frame's place, section 6.2); it may leave to the chain the values that are functions of the
ciphertext (`decoded_length`, `payload_digest`, the identifier), because a change of those without a change of the ciphertext
changes nothing a key holder decrypts. Corollary F (14.3) states this as a theorem; section 10 states what a keyless party sees.

1. `P8-1` A segment that holds a frame of type `keys` or `sealed`, or a value that this part adds to any enumeration, MUST declare
   `registry_version` `2`.
2. `P8-2` This part MUST NOT be read as adding a field to, or changing a rule of, any record or procedure of registry version `1`;
   every rule of this part applies to the frame types, inner types and values it adds.

## 2. Primitives

Every primitive the construction uses, with the exact standard, the parameters fixed here, where it is used and the security
notion that the proofs assume of it. A notion marked ASSUMPTION is numbered in section 19 with what the cited texts do and do not
establish.

- SHA-256. standard and parameters: FIPS 180-4, "Secure Hash Standard", August 2015; 32-byte output, 64-byte block; used for:
  inside HMAC; notion assumed: collision resistance (assumption `S-0`, Theorem C and Lemma K); through HMAC, the pseudo-random
  properties of its compression function that assumptions `S-1` and `S-2` state (Theorem E and Lemma K).
- HMAC-SHA-256, written `HMAC`. standard and parameters: RFC 2104, "HMAC: Keyed-Hashing for Message Authentication", February
  1997, and FIPS 198-1, "The Keyed-Hash Message Authentication Code (HMAC)", July 2008 (in force; NIST has proposed its withdrawal
  in favour of SP 800-224, still a draft); every HMAC key of sections 5 and 6 is exactly 32 bytes, so never longer than the block;
  HPKE's internal HMAC keys (9.2) are as RFC 9180 fixes them, one of them empty, and lie outside Theorem C; used for: the
  synthetic value `sv` (6.3); inside HKDF; notion assumed: pseudo-random function under a uniform 32-byte key (assumption `S-2`);
  collision resistance over (key, message) pairs with keys of one length follows from that of SHA-256 by Lemma H (12.2).
- HKDF-SHA-256. standard and parameters: RFC 5869, "HMAC-based Extract-and-Expand Key Derivation Function (HKDF)", May 2010,
  sections 2.2 and 2.3; salt the 32 bytes `0^32` (the default of section 2.2 for an absent salt), output length 32; used for: the
  key schedule (5); notion assumed: the two derived keys are pseudo-random and independent from a uniform 32-byte input key
  (assumption `S-1`).
- AES-256-GCM-SIV. standard and parameters: RFC 8452, "AES-GCM-SIV: Nonce Misuse-Resistant Authenticated Encryption", April 2019,
  `AEAD_AES_256_GCM_SIV`: key 32 bytes, nonce 12 bytes, tag 16 bytes, plaintext and associated data each at most `2^36` bytes (its
  section 6); used for: the sealing cipher (6.4); notion assumed: multi-user misuse-resistant authenticated encryption, with
  AES-256 modelled as an ideal cipher (assumption `S-3`). It is TIDY, a fact of the specification and not an assumption:
  decryption that accepts recomputes the tag from the plaintext it returns (RFC 8452, section 5), so encrypting that plaintext
  under the same key, nonce and associated data gives back the ciphertext.
- BLAKE3 key-derivation mode, written `H_ctx`. standard and parameters: the container's construction (`A-1`); 32-byte output; used
  for: identifiers and payload digests (container); the recipient key identifier (9.2); notion assumed: none in Theorems C and E,
  Lemma K or Corollary W; collision resistance where the container claims it of identifiers and digests, and for the binding of a
  recipient key identifier to a public key (15, items 13 and 16).
- X-Wing. standard and parameters: `draft-connolly-cfrg-xwing-kem-11`, "X-Wing: general-purpose hybrid post-quantum KEM", 23
  September 2026: ML-KEM-768 of FIPS 203 combined with X25519 of RFC 7748; encapsulation key 1,216 bytes, ciphertext 1,120 bytes,
  shared secret 32 bytes, decapsulation key a 32-byte seed; used for: the KEM of the key wrap (9); notion assumed: IND-CCA key
  encapsulation in the random-oracle model for SHA3-256, SHA3-512 and SHAKE-256, as that draft states it (assumption `S-4`).
- HPKE base mode. standard and parameters: RFC 9180, "Hybrid Public Key Encryption", February 2022, sections 4, 5.1, 5.1.1, 5.2
  and 6.1: mode `0x00`, KEM `0x647A` (X-Wing), KDF `0x0001` (HKDF-SHA256), AEAD `0x0002` (AES-256-GCM, key 32, nonce 12, tag 16
  bytes); single-shot interface; used for: the key wrap (9); notion: labelled IND-CCA public-key encryption with `info` as the
  label (11.4), PROVED in Lemma K from `S-0`, `S-2`, `S-4` and `S-5`.
- AES-256-GCM. standard and parameters: NIST SP 800-38D, "Recommendation for Block Cipher Modes of Operation: Galois/Counter Mode
  (GCM) and GMAC", November 2007, inside HPKE only, as RFC 9180 fixes it; used for: the key wrap; notion assumed: one-time
  authenticated encryption under many independent keys (assumption `S-5`); never a sealing cipher.

Two hashes stand in this part, and each has one role. BLAKE3, through `H_ctx`, is the container's hash of public input. SHA-256 is
the hash of every KEYED function of the seal: the key schedule and the synthetic value. The reason is that the theorems reduce to
properties that published results state for HMAC and HKDF: collision resistance of HMAC over pairs follows from collision
resistance of its hash by an argument written out in full here (Lemma H), and the pseudo-randomness of HMAC under a uniform key is
the subject of published theorems (assumption `S-2`). The keyed and key-derivation modes of BLAKE3 have no published statement of
either property with the key as an input (remark `R-1`). With SHA-256 in every keyed role, Theorem C rests on collision resistance
of SHA-256 alone and on nothing of BLAKE3.

1. `P8-3` A reader of registry version `2` MUST implement every primitive of this section, with exactly the parameters it states.
2. `P8-4` Every HMAC key of sections 5 and 6 MUST be exactly 32 bytes long, and a later registry version that keeps this
   construction MUST NOT admit an HMAC key of another length in them; Lemma H, on which Theorem C rests, is false for keys of two
   lengths or longer than the block (12.2).

## 4. The key table: the `keys` frame

### 4.1 Segment keys

A SEGMENT KEY `SK` is 32 bytes. It is generated outside the format, by a cryptographic random generator or by a key-derivation
function with its own inputs, and it reaches a reader either from the reader's own key input (4.4) or by a recipient entry (9). It
is never used directly: the key schedule derives the two keys the seal uses (5). A key is named in a file by its KEY IDENTIFIER, a
32-byte value chosen by whoever generates the key and independent of `SK` (for example 32 random bytes, or a digest of a name in
the owner's key management).

1. `P8-7` A segment key MUST be 32 bytes drawn uniformly at random or produced by a key-derivation function from a secret of at
   least 256 bits of entropy. A segment key derived from a password or another low-entropy secret is outside every claim of this
   part (13.6).
2. `P8-8` A key identifier MUST NOT be computed from `SK` or from any value derived from `SK`.

### 4.2 The records

```text
keys.content    = [ entry_count: uint, recipient_count: uint ]
keys_payload    = [ entries: list(key_entry), recipients: list(recipient_entry) ]
key_entry       = [ key_id: bytes32, cipher: enum(sealing_cipher), context: bytes ]
recipient_entry = [ suite: enum(key_wrap_suite), recipient_key_id: digest, target_key_id: bytes32,
                    encapsulation: bytes, wrapped: bytes ]
```

The decoded payload of a `keys` frame is `E(keys_payload)`. `entry_count` and `recipient_count` are the lengths of the two lists,
so that a reader bounds its allocation from the envelope. The `context` of a key entry is the KEY CONTEXT: bytes chosen by the
writer, an input and never randomness, which separates two files sealed under one key (6.2, `P8-29`). `recipient_entry` is defined
in section 9.

1. `P8-9` `entry_count` MUST equal the length of `entries` and `recipient_count` the length of `recipients`; a disagreement is
   `malformed_value` with reason `inconsistent_counts`.
2. `P8-10` `entry_count + recipient_count` MUST be at least `1`; a `keys` frame with no entry is `malformed_value` with reason
   `out_of_range`.
3. `P8-11` `context` MUST be at most `64` bytes long (the constant `max_key_context`, 7.1); a longer one is `malformed_value` with
   reason `wrong_length`.
4. `P8-12` A `keys` frame MUST be stored `stored_plain`; another storage is `malformed_value` with reason
    `forbidden_combination`.
5. `P8-57` The list `recipients` MUST be in strictly ascending bytewise order of the pair `E(recipient_key_id)`, then
    `E(target_key_id)` (the bytes compared as unsigned integers from the first byte, a proper prefix before the longer string);
    a recipient entry whose pair is equal to or below the pair of the entry before it in the list is `malformed_value` with
    reason `unordered_or_repeated` for the entry (9.3). The pair is a total order on the entries a frame admits, since two
    entries with one pair cannot both exist (`P8-50`).
6. `P8-59` A reader MUST judge a `keys` frame whose link state is `linked` or `healed` in this order, and the first violation is
    the one `malformed_value` of the frame:
    the storage (`P8-12`); the decoded payload against `keys_payload`, the first violation in the order of its bytes with the
    reason `P1` gives; `entry_count` against the length of `entries` and then `recipient_count` against the length of
    `recipients` (`P8-9`); the sum at least `1` (`P8-10`); the `context` of each entry in list order (`P8-11`); the `key_id` of
    each entry in list order (`P8-14`); the layout of the frame, in the order of `P8-61` (the number of entries, then the next
    item, then an earlier use of the key index). The layout is judged last of the rules of the frame because it needs the table
    that the earlier rules leave and the envelope fields of the items that follow, and before the rules of one recipient entry. The
    rules of one recipient entry (9.3) are judged only on a frame that has no violation of these.

### 4.3 The key table of a segment

The KEY TABLE of a segment is the list of the key entries of its `keys` frames, in file order: the entries of the first applied
`keys` frame, then those of the next, each in list order. The KEY INDEX of an entry is its zero-based position in the table. A
segment's table is its own: nothing of it is inherited from another segment.

1. `P8-13` A `keys` frame MUST be applied whole or not at all. A `keys` frame whose link is broken, whose payload is damaged or
    unchecked, or which breaks any rule of 4.2 or of this subsection (judged in the order of `P8-59`, one finding for the frame; the
    rules of one recipient entry, `P8-48`, `P8-49` and `P8-57`, cost that entry only), adds no entry to the table, and none of its recipient entries is tried.
2. `P8-14` The `key_id` of a key entry MUST differ from the `key_id` of every entry already in the table and of every other entry
    of the same frame; a repeated `key_id` is `malformed_value` with reason `unordered_or_repeated`, and the frame is not applied.
3. `P8-15` A key entry takes the next key index when its frame is applied; an entry is never replaced, and rotation is a new
    entry.

   A sealed frame is UNDER a key entry when its `key_index` names the entry, judged on the envelope alone as `P8-49` judges it,
   whatever the frame's link, payload or outcome. THE FIRST SEALED FRAME UNDER AN ENTRY is the one with the lowest position in the
   segment. The FIRST USE of a key entry is the position of that frame; an entry has no first use when no sealed frame is under it.
   A sealed frame names one key index, so no two entries have one first use.

4. `P8-58` A writer MUST write the key entries of a segment in ascending order of first use, and MUST NOT write a key entry
    that no sealed frame of the segment is under. A writer MUST write the `keys` frame that holds an entry immediately before
    the first sealed frame under it, with no other ordinary frame between them (a transparent frame, which `P5` places by its own
    rule, may stand between). That frame holds exactly that one entry, and holds as its
    recipient entries all the recipient entries whose target is that entry, in the order of `P8-57`; the recipient entries of a
    key are therefore all in the frame that holds the key. The key index of an entry is then the number of distinct key
    entries whose first use is below its own.
5. `P8-61` A reader MUST judge the layout of `P8-58` on every `keys` frame whose link state is `linked` or `healed`, and MUST
    report a `keys` frame that breaks it as `malformed_value` with the reason `unordered_or_repeated`, the reason this part gives
    to an item that is out of its order or not in its place (`P8-14`, `P8-49`, `P8-57`). Let `n` be the key index that the entry
    of the frame takes if the frame is applied. A `keys` frame BREAKS THE LAYOUT when it holds other than exactly one key entry;
    or the next item of the segment
    after it that is not a transparent frame is not a frame of type `sealed` whose `key_index` is `n`; or a frame of type `sealed`
    that stands earlier in the segment has the `key_index` `n`. The judgement is made on the envelope fields alone, as `P8-49`
    judges a first use, whatever the link, payload or outcome of the frames. An entry that no sealed frame is under, an entry
    out of the order of first use and a recipient entry that stands in another frame than the frame of its target are thereby
    refused: the first has no sealed frame next after its frame, and the second names a key index other than `n` in the frame
    that follows; the third is refused by `P8-49`. A `keys` frame that breaks the layout is treated as every `keys` frame with a
    `malformed_value` is (`P8-13`): it adds no entry, and none of its recipient entries is tried.

### 4.4 The reader's key input

A reader's key input for sealing is a value of its own, given to it beside the file:

```text
sealing_input = [ segment_keys: list([ key_id: bytes32, segment_key: bytes32 ]),
                  recipient_keys: list([ suite: enum(key_wrap_suite), recipient_key_id: digest, seed: bytes32 ]) ]
```

`seed` is the recipient's decapsulation key in the seed form of its suite (9.1). A reader HOLDS A KEY for a key entry when
`segment_keys` lists its `key_id`, and HOLDS A RECIPIENT KEY for a recipient entry when `recipient_keys` lists its `suite` and
`recipient_key_id`.

A `sealing_input` is VALID exactly when all of these hold:

- no two entries of `segment_keys` have one `key_id`;
- no two entries of `recipient_keys` have one `suite` and one `recipient_key_id`;
- for each entry of `recipient_keys`, the `suite` is a value of `key_wrap_suite` of this registry version and `recipient_key_id`
  equals `H_ctx(recipient_key_id, E([suite, public_key]))` of the public key that the suite derives from the entry's `seed` (9.1).

Two byte-equal entries are two entries with one `key_id`, or with one `suite` and one `recipient_key_id`: a duplicate is invalid
whether or not its entries are equal, so no reader chooses among duplicates, and no policy of first or last applies.

AN INVALID INPUT IS REFUSED. A reader that is given a `sealing_input` that is not valid returns the outcome `sealing_input_refused`
in place of a result of reading. It is an outcome of the reader's interface, not a finding of a file: it has no finding code, no
severity and no place in the typed result of reading (`P7`), and it does not depend on the file. Its fields are `reason`, a value
of the enumeration `sealing_refusal_reason` of `P2` (`duplicate_segment_key`, `duplicate_recipient_key`,
`recipient_key_mismatch`), and `index: uint`, the zero-based position in its list
of the first entry that makes the input invalid, found by judging the entries of `segment_keys` in order and then those of
`recipient_keys` in order, an entry being invalid when it repeats an earlier entry of its list or fails the third rule above (a
`suite` that this registry does not define is `recipient_key_mismatch`). At one entry the rules are judged in the order in which
this section lists them (the first rule for `segment_keys`; the second, then the third, for `recipient_keys`), and the first that
the entry breaks gives the `reason`: an entry of `recipient_keys` that repeats an earlier entry and also fails the third rule is
`duplicate_recipient_key`. The reader makes this judgment before it reads the file:
it opens no frame, unwraps no recipient entry and reports no finding.

1. `P8-16` Two readers of registry version `2` with equal `sealing_input` and equal capacity MUST give the same result for the
    same file; the result MUST NOT depend on any other key material. `sealing_input` holds secrets and is no field of the
    result of reading.
2. `P8-54` A reader MUST judge the validity of its `sealing_input` by the three rules of 4.4, in their order, before it reads the
    file, and MUST return `sealing_input_refused` with the reason and the index of 4.4 for an input that is not valid, whatever the file holds. It
    MUST NOT choose among entries that share a `key_id`, or a `suite` with a `recipient_key_id`, and MUST NOT ignore, replace or
    combine such entries.
3. `P8-55` A reader MUST NOT report `sealing_input_refused` as a finding, MUST NOT put it in the typed result of reading, and MUST
    NOT let it depend on the bytes of the file.
4. `P8-56` A reader MUST check each `recipient_keys` entry by deriving the public key from its `seed` by the key generation of
    the suite (9.1), computing its `recipient_key_id` by `H_ctx(recipient_key_id, E([suite, public_key]))`, and comparing it with
    the entry's `recipient_key_id`.

## 5. The key schedule

The key schedule derives, from one segment key `SK` and the cipher of its key entry, the two keys of the seal: `K_aead`, the key
of AES-256-GCM-SIV, and `K_commit`, the key of the synthetic value. It is HKDF with SHA-256 (RFC 5869):

```text
PRK      = HKDF-Extract(salt = 0^32, IKM = SK)                       = HMAC(0^32, SK)
info(p)  = E([ cipher: enum(sealing_cipher), purpose: enum(seal_key_purpose) ])   with purpose p
K_aead   = HKDF-Expand(PRK, info(aead),   32)                        = HMAC(PRK, info(aead)   || 0x01)
K_commit = HKDF-Expand(PRK, info(commit), 32)                        = HMAC(PRK, info(commit) || 0x01)
```

The right-hand forms are RFC 5869 written out: the extract step is HMAC keyed by the salt over `IKM`, and an output of 32 bytes is
the first block `T(1) = HMAC(PRK, info || 0x01)` of the expansion (its section 2.3). With the one cipher of version `2`,
`info(aead)` is the three bytes `82 01 01` and `info(commit)` the three bytes `82 01 02`, so the two HMAC messages are
`82 01 01 01` and `82 01 02 01`. Three HMAC calls derive both keys; each has a 32-byte key (`0^32` and `PRK`), as `P8-4` requires.

The schedule has no input but `SK` and the cipher. The header, the file, the key context and the frame's place are not in it; they
are in the associated data of each frame (6.2). So one segment key gives the same two keys in every file, and what separates files
under one key is the associated data. The cipher is in `info` so that a later registry version that adds a sealing cipher derives
keys independent of these from the same `SK`.

1. `P8-17` A writer and a reader MUST derive `K_aead` and `K_commit` exactly as above, with the `cipher` of the key entry.
2. `P8-18` `SK` MUST NOT be used except as the input key material of this schedule and as the plaintext of a recipient entry (9);
   `PRK`, `K_aead` and `K_commit` MUST NOT be used except in this schedule, the AEAD and the synthetic value of this part. In
   particular `SK` MUST NOT be an AES key, an HMAC key, a KEM key or a signing key.

## 6. The sealed frame

### 6.1 The clear envelope

A sealed frame is a frame of the container (`A-1`) with these field values:

| field | value |
|---|---|
| `frame_type` | `sealed` (18) |
| `critical`, `transparent` | `true`, `false` |
| `content` | `sealed_content = [ key_index: uint, ordinal: uint, sv: bytes32 ]` |
| `decoded_length` | `len(C)`, the length of the ciphertext |
| `payload_digest` | `H_ctx(payload, C)` |
| `storage` | `stored_plain` |
| `payload` | `C`, the ciphertext with its 16-byte tag |

`key_index` names the key entry of the segment's table that seals the frame; `ordinal` is the frame's own ordinal; `sv` is the
synthetic value (6.3). This is the one place where `decoded_length` and `payload_digest` are defined over bytes that are not a
decoded plaintext: for a sealed frame the "decoded payload" of the container is the ciphertext, so a keyless party checks the
stored bytes against the identifier (`A-2`, `A-3`) without a key and without a codec.

1. `P8-19` A sealed frame MUST have the field values of the table. The frame type and the columns `critical` and `transparent`
    are judged by `P8-6` and by the rule of `P1` on the registry columns, at the step ITEMS of reading, and not here;
    `decoded_length`, `payload_digest` and `payload` are judged by step 1 of 7.2, which takes the stored bytes as `C`, with no
    codec, and by the container's stored check, which runs on it as on any frame that carries one. This rule judges, in this
    order, the `content` against `sealed_content` (the first violation in the order of its bytes, with the reason `P1` gives) and
    then the `storage`: a sealed frame whose `storage` is not `stored_plain` is `malformed_value` with reason
    `forbidden_combination`.
2. `P8-20` `key_index` MUST be smaller than the number of entries the segment's key table holds when the sealed frame is met; a
    larger one is `malformed_value` with reason `out_of_range`.
3. `P8-21` `ordinal` MUST equal the frame's ordinal; a frame whose `ordinal` differs is `malformed_value` with reason
    `sealed_ordinal`. The check needs no key.
4. `P8-22` `len(C)` MUST be at least `17` and at most `2^27 + 16`; another length is `malformed_value` with reason
    `out_of_range`. The check needs no key, so every reader whose capacity admits the frame refuses the same frames; a frame
    above a reader's capacity is `exceeds_capacity` at step 1 of 7.2 and is not judged further.

### 6.2 The associated data

```text
seal_ad = [ header_identifier: digest, key_id: bytes32, context: bytes, key_index: uint, ordinal: uint ]
AD      = E(seal_ad)
```

`header_identifier` is the `h0` of the frame's segment; `key_id` and `context` are those of the key entry that `key_index` names.
`AD` is the associated data given to the cipher, whole: it is at most 153 bytes (`1 + 34 + 34 + 66 + 9 + 9`), ten blocks of 16
bytes, and AES-256-GCM-SIV accepts it as it is.

What `AD` binds, and why each field is there: `h0` binds the frame to its segment and through it to every field of the opener, the
lineage included, so a frame lifted to another segment does not open; `key_id` binds the key entry; `context` separates two files
whose first segments have equal headers under one key; `key_index` and `ordinal` bind the frame to its place, so a frame moved
within its segment, or to another key index, does not open. Not in `AD`: `sv` (an output of the seal), `decoded_length` and
`payload_digest` (functions of the ciphertext, which would make the ciphertext depend on itself), the frame type and flags
(constants of the type, fixed by `P8-6`), the predecessor (a damaged identifier of one frame must not make the next frame
unopenable), and `storage`, `payload` and `signatures` (outside the identifier, `A-2`). A change to any of these that leaves the
ciphertext unchanged changes nothing that a key holder decrypts.

`E(seal_ad)` is a definite-length CBOR array, a self-delimiting item: no encoding of a `seal_ad` is a proper prefix of another
string that begins with an encoding of a `seal_ad`. So `AD || P` determines `AD` and `P` (used in 6.3 and Theorem C), and `AD`
determines `seal_ad`.

1. `P8-23` A writer and a reader MUST build `AD` from the segment's `h0`, the key entry named by `key_index`, and the `key_index`
    and `ordinal` of the sealed content, and from nothing else.

### 6.3 The synthetic value and the nonce

```text
sv    = HMAC(K_commit, AD || P)          ; 32 bytes, stored whole in sealed_content
nonce = sv[0..12]
```

`P` is the padded plaintext (6.5). The one value `sv` has two roles: its first 12 bytes are the nonce of the cipher, and the whole
32 bytes are the commitment to `(SK, AD, P)` that the reader recomputes after decryption. Theorem C is the claim for the second
role and uses nothing of the cipher; Theorem E is the claim for the first role and for the whole construction.

1. `P8-24` `sv` MUST be stored and compared whole: a writer and a reader MUST NOT truncate it, and a reader MUST compare all 32
    bytes, in time that does not depend on where the first difference stands.

### 6.4 Sealing a frame

Input: the segment's `h0`; a key entry `[key_id, cipher, context]` at key index `k` with its segment key `SK`; the ordinal `o` of
the frame to be written; the inner frame (6.6).

1. Build the sealed inner record `inner` of the frame (6.6) and `P = pad(E(inner))` (6.5). If `len(P)` exceeds `2^27`, refuse:
   write nothing.
2. If `2^32` frames have been sealed under `SK` by all its holders together (counting every frame sealed under it in any file,
   cut tails and repeated renderings included), refuse: write nothing.
3. Derive `K_aead` and `K_commit` (5).
4. `AD = E([h0, key_id, context, k, o])`.
5. `sv = HMAC(K_commit, AD || P)`; `nonce = sv[0..12]`.
6. `C = AES-256-GCM-SIV.Encrypt(K_aead, nonce, AD, P)`, the ciphertext followed by the 16-byte tag (RFC 8452, section 4), so
   `len(C) = len(P) + 16`.
7. Write the frame of 6.1 with `sealed_content = [k, o, sv]`, `decoded_length = len(C)`, `payload_digest = H_ctx(payload, C)`,
   `payload = C`, and its identifier (`A-1`).

Nothing is random: the same key, segment, key entry, ordinal and inner frame give the same bytes. A writer's two refusals are
errors of the call that asked for the frame; they are not outcomes of reading.

1. `P8-25` A writer MUST seal by exactly these steps, and MUST NOT write a sealed frame that step 1 or step 2 refuses.
2. `P8-26` The count of step 2 MUST be the count of every frame sealed under the segment key by ANY of its holders, and a
    holder that cannot establish that count MUST take a new segment key. A recipient that did not generate a segment key MUST NOT
    seal under it unless the holders keep one count and give their files distinct key contexts.
3. `P8-27` A writer MUST write the `keys` frame that holds a key entry before the first sealed frame under that entry.
4. `P8-28` A segment that holds a frame of type `keys` or `sealed` MUST have `canonical` `absent` and MUST NOT hold an `index`
    frame; a violation is `malformed_value` with reason `forbidden_combination`, reported once for the header, at position `0`, when
    `canonical` is present (the header staying readable), and once at each `index` frame of the segment; a segment with both
    gets all of these findings. Its `offsets` item stays allowed; with no `index` frame the segment has no locator.
5. `P8-29` A writer MUST NOT seal under one segment key two segments that have equal `h0` and equal key contexts. A writer that
    seals several files under one key gives each file's key entry a different `context`; it takes it from a name or counter it
    holds, and MUST NOT take it from a digest of content.

### 6.5 Padding: the one policy

Every sealed plaintext is padded by the function `padme` (the Padmé function of Nikitin and others, "Reducing Metadata Leakage
from Encrypted Files and Communication with PURBs", Proceedings on Privacy Enhancing Technologies 2019, Algorithm 1), stated here
in full so that nothing depends on the reference. For a length `n`:

```text
n < 2:   padme(n) = n
n >= 2:  e = floor(log2(n)),  s = floor(log2(e)) + 1,  z = e - s,  padme(n) = ceil(n / 2^z) * 2^z
pad(x) = x || 0^(padme(len(x)) - len(x))
```

`padme(n)` is the smallest multiple of `2^z` that is not below `n`: the length keeps its `s` leading bits and the rest are rounded
up. Examples: `padme(82) = 88`, `padme(100) = 104`, `padme(129) = 144`, `padme(1000) = 1024`, `padme(2^27) = 2^27`. The overhead
is at most `15/129` of the length (about 11.6 per cent, at `n = 129`), at most 6.25 per cent from 257 bytes and at most 3.125 per
cent from 65,537 bytes. For lengths from `1` to `2^27` there are 524 padded lengths, so a padded length tells at most about nine
bits of the length it hides. Every `n` up to `2^27` pads to at most `2^27`, because `2^27` is a multiple of every `2^z` that
arises below it.

One policy, applied to every sealed frame, with no parameter: a pad policy per key would be a writer choice that leaks which
policy was chosen and gives two encodings of one plaintext.

1. `P8-30` The plaintext of every sealed frame MUST be `pad(E(inner))`.
2. `P8-31` A reader MUST decode from `P` one complete deterministic item as `inner` (6.6), and then MUST require that the
    remaining bytes number exactly `padme(n) - n`, where `n` is the length of that item, and are all zero. A violation of the
    second requirement is `malformed_value` with reason `padding`. A first item that does not decode as `inner` is judged by 6.6
    first.

### 6.6 The sealed inner record

```text
sealed_inner   = [ inner_type: enum(sealed_inner_type), content: inner_content, decoded_length: uint,
                   payload_digest: digest, storage: sealed_storage, payload: bytes ]
sealed_storage = [1]                         ; stored_plain: payload is the decoded payload
               / [2, codec: enum(codec)]     ; stored_compressed: payload is the codec output over the decoded payload
```

`sealed_inner` is a variant on `sealed_inner_type`: its tag decides the record `inner_content` and the decoded payload, which are
those that the table of `sealed_inner_type` (3.2) names: the content record and decoded payload of the frame type of the same
label in registry version `1`, and for `sealed_members` those of 8.3. `decoded_length` and `payload_digest` are the inner frame's
own: the length of its decoded payload `d` and `H_ctx(payload, d)`, exactly as for a clear frame of that type. `sealed_storage`
takes its tags from `storage_kind`; it has no stored check, because the cipher authenticates every byte. A compressed inner
payload obeys every rule that the container sets for the compressed payload of a clear frame.

Compression happens before sealing and is chosen per frame inside the seal. Compressing in one frame a secret together with data
an adversary influences lets an observer of lengths learn about the secret (10.2); padding narrows that channel and does not close
it.

1. `P8-32` `inner` MUST be a value of `sealed_inner` under the typed rules of section 0; a value outside it (a wrong length, an
   unregistered tag, a field of the wrong type, a non-deterministic encoding) is `malformed_value` with the reason of the rule
   broken.
2. `P8-33` `inner_content` MUST be the content record of the type its tag names and MUST satisfy every rule that registry
    version `1` sets for that type; a violation is reported with the outcome a clear frame of that type gets. In a segment
    whose `registry_version` is above the reader's, an enumeration value of `inner_content` that the reader does not hold is
    no violation: it is judged after step 9 of 7.2 has passed, as the values of a clear frame are judged after its payload, and
    the inner frame gets the outcome of `P2-62`; every inner type is critical, so the sealed frame gives `unsupported_critical`
    with the subject that a clear frame of the inner type gives, stops the fold of the segment, every later frame is `stopped`,
    the sealed frame is opaque with `unsupported_critical` and it counts in `unopened_sealed`.
3. `P8-34` A writer MUST NOT store in the clear, in the same segment, a frame that it also seals: a frame is either clear or
    sealed.

### 6.7 The inner frame

When a sealed frame opens and its inner record passes every check (7.2), the INNER FRAME is a frame of the type that `inner_type`
names, with the content record, decoded length, payload digest and decoded payload of `inner`, standing at the sealed frame's
position, with the sealed frame's identifier and predecessor. It is folded, and checked, by the rules of its type exactly as a
clear frame of that type would be at that position; its `critical` and `transparent` columns are those of the type in the
registry. An inner `suppress` frame is a folded `suppress` frame like any other, and is authenticated exactly when the sealed
frame that carries it is an authenticated frame.

## 7. Opening a sealed frame

### 7.1 Constants

- `max_sealed_plaintext`. value: `2^27` bytes of padded plaintext `P`; what fixes it: the AEAD term of Theorem E at the per-key
  limit (13.5): with this length and `max_seals_per_key`, one key contributes at most `2^-42.33`.
- `max_seals_per_key`. value: `2^32` frames sealed under one segment key by all its holders, over all files; what fixes it:
  Theorem E at the per-key limit (13.5): the triple-nonce term is then `2^-98.59` and the AEAD term `2^-42.33` per key.
- `max_failed_openings`. value: `2^10` openings per segment key, within one read; what fixes it: the forgery cost of Theorem E
  (13.5): each failed opening of full length adds at most `2^-74.33` to the AEAD term, so `2^10` of them add at most `2^-64.33`.
- `max_key_context`. value: `64` bytes; what fixes it: bounds `AD` at 153 bytes, ten blocks, the figure the AEAD term uses (13.5).

### 7.2 The procedure

Input: a frame of type `sealed`, the segment's `h0` and key table, and the reader's `sealing_input`. Every step runs in this
order; the first that ends the procedure gives the outcome, and later steps do not run.

1. CONTAINER CHECKS. The link and the payload check of the container run first and need no key (`A-3`): the payload check compares
   `decoded_length` with `len(payload)` and `payload_digest` with `H_ctx(payload, the stored payload bytes)`. A payload that fails
   is `damaged_frame` with its reason; the frame is not opened and no opening is counted. A frame whose link state is `broken` is
   not judged by steps 2 to 10: its content is not interpreted, it is not opened, and no opening is counted.
2. CLEAR CONTENT. `P8-19` to `P8-22` are judged in the order of their numbers, and the first that the frame breaks gives the one
   `malformed_value` of the frame, with its reason; the frame is not opened.
3. KEY. The key entry is the one at `key_index`. Its segment key is resolved by 9.4. If no segment key results, the frame is not
   opened: opaque with reason `missing_key`, and the outcome is `segment_key_not_held` or `key_conflict` as 9.4 gives it.
4. COUNT. The sealed frames are opened in ascending position within a segment and the segments in file order, and the count runs
   in that order. If the reader has counted `max_failed_openings` failed openings under this segment key in this read, the frame is not
   opened: opaque with reason `exceeds_capacity`, finding `exceeds_capacity` with reason `failed_openings`.
5. DERIVE. `K_aead`, `K_commit` (5); `AD` (6.2); `nonce = sv[0..12]`.
6. DECRYPT. `P = AES-256-GCM-SIV.Decrypt(K_aead, nonce, AD, C)`. If the cipher refuses: `seal_failed` with reason `aead`; the
   frame is opaque with reason `damaged`, its payload state `damaged`, and one failed opening is counted.
7. COMMITMENT. `sv' = HMAC(K_commit, AD || P)`; if `sv'` differs from `sv` (`P8-24`): `seal_failed` with reason `commitment`; `P`
   is discarded unread; the frame is opaque with reason `damaged`, its payload state `damaged`, and one failed opening is counted.
8. INNER. Judge, in this order, `P8-32` (the first item of `P` against `sealed_inner`, the first violation in the order of its
   bytes), then `P8-31` (the padding after that item), then `P8-33` (the rules of the inner type). The first that fails gives
   the one `malformed_value`, with its reason (a `P` that holds no complete item: `wrong_length`); the frame is opaque with reason
   `malformed`.
9. INNER PAYLOAD. Check the inner payload by steps 1 to 7 of the payload check of `P1` ("The payload check", 5.4), in their
   order, applied to the inner `storage`, `payload`, `decoded_length` and `payload_digest`; the first step that fails decides.
   Beyond the reader's capacity the outcome is `exceeds_capacity` with the reason that step 1 of that check gives
   (`decoded_size`, or `total_decoded` when the inner `decoded_length` would bring the total decoded length of the read
   above the reader's limit), the frame opaque with reason `exceeds_capacity` and its payload state `unchecked`. Otherwise the payload is decoded by `storage` and the inner
   `decoded_length` and `payload_digest` are checked. A failure is `damaged_frame` with reason `decode_failed`,
   `decoded_length` or `payload_digest`, the reason of the first failing step; the frame is opaque with reason `damaged` and its
   payload state `damaged`.
10. FOLD. The inner frame (6.7) is folded at the frame's position.

A sealed frame that is not opened at step 3 or 4 keeps the payload state of step 1 and is counted in `unopened_sealed`, the count
of sealed frames not opened that the result of reading reports; so is a frame not opened at step 1 or 2, and a frame whose
opening ends at steps 6 to 9: the count is of every sealed frame that is not folded.

1. `P8-35` A reader of registry version `2` MUST open every sealed frame by the steps of 7.2, in that order.
2. `P8-36` A reader MUST NOT expose, fold or keep any byte of `P` before step 7 has passed, and MUST NOT fold any part of the
    inner frame before step 9 has passed.
3. `P8-37` A reader MUST count, per segment key and within one read, the sealed frames that end at step 6 or step 7, and MUST
    stop opening frames under that key when the count reaches `max_failed_openings` (step 4), the frames being opened in
    ascending position within a segment and the segments in file order. A frame that ends at an earlier step
    is not counted, so damage in storage, which step 1 finds, never uses up the count.
4. `P8-38` A reader without a usable key MUST still run step 1 on every sealed frame and step 2 on every sealed frame whose link
    state is `linked` or `healed`, and MUST continue with the next item;
    a sealed frame that is not opened MUST NOT stop the fold of the segment.

### 7.3 What each outcome says

`seal_failed` with `aead` says that `(nonce, AD, C)` is not a ciphertext of the cipher under `K_aead`; with `commitment`, that the
cipher accepted it and the recomputed synthetic value differs. A keyless party that submits frames and observes which of the two a
reader reports learns whether a triple it chose is accepted by the bare cipher: a verification oracle for AES-256-GCM-SIV. The
game of Theorem E gives the adversary exactly that oracle (11.3), and the bound pays for it, so the two reasons stay distinct. A
failure after step 7 (steps 8 and 9) is reported only for a frame that the cipher and the commitment accepted, so it is no oracle
for a party without the key. AES-256-GCM-SIV does not commit to its key: anyone who knows a set of candidate keys can build one
`(nonce, AD, C)` that the bare cipher accepts under each of them, and a reader's report of `commitment` rather than `aead` then
tells the submitter that the reader's key is in the set. With uniform keys this is of no use; with guessable keys the two reasons
are a partitioning oracle, and such keys are outside every claim (`P8-7`).

## 8. Sealed packs and the sealed member index

### 8.1 The unit of sealing for blobs is the pack

Blob bytes are sealed only as SEALED PACKS: sealed frames whose inner type is `pack`. The inner pack is a pack of registry version
`1`, with its directory and bodies, so every member's kind, content record, digest and length is inside the ciphertext.
Chunking, deduplication and compression happen on the plaintext before sealing, so a key holder deduplicates units within a key as
in a clear file, and nothing about the units is visible but the padded length of each pack. No keyed chunker and no keyed locator
exist: a unit's boundaries and digest are those of the clear format, and they are never in the clear for a sealed unit.

1. `P8-39` A writer MUST NOT write a `chunk`, `blob`, `recipe` or `value` frame of a unit or blob that it seals: the unit is a
    member of a sealed pack, and the pack is the sealed frame.
2. `P8-40` A key holder MUST include every member of every sealed pack it opened in the unit index used to rebuild blobs and
    values (the container's rebuilding of blobs and values), at the sealed frame's ordinal, exactly as a member of a clear pack.
3. `P8-63` A key holder MUST charge the `recipe` and `value` members of a sealed pack it opened to the total rebuilt length of
    the read (the container's "The total rebuilt length") at the position of the sealed frame in item order, in directory order,
    before it rebuilds any of them, and a reader MUST charge nothing for a sealed pack it did not open. `P8-42` is judged on the
    entries of a refused declaration as on those of an admitted one: it reads the digests the entries name and no byte.

### 8.2 Seal scope

A clear frame that names, by its digest, a unit held only inside sealed packs discloses that digest to every party, who can then
test a guess of the sealed content.

1. `P8-41` A writer MUST NOT write a clear frame whose recipe, value or member entry names a unit digest that only sealed packs
    of the segment hold.
2. `P8-42` A key holder that resolves an entry of a clear `recipe` or `value` frame, or of a clear pack member, only to a unit
    held inside a sealed pack MUST report `seal_scope_violated` at the clear frame, once for that frame; the fold is unchanged.

### 8.3 The sealed member index

Opening a sealed frame authenticates all of it (7.2), so finding one unit in a segment of sealed packs without an index costs the
opening of every pack. The SEALED MEMBER INDEX is a sealed frame of inner type `sealed_members`, under one key index, that lists
the members of that key's sealed packs. A segment that holds sealed frames has no clear `index` frame (`P8-28`), because the clear
index's statistics, graph directory, content digest and pack members are functions of plaintext.

```text
sealed_members.content = [ first: uint, entry_count: uint ]
sealed_members payload = list(member_entry)          ; the decoded payload is E of this list
member_entry           = [ unit_digest: digest, ordinal: uint, member_index: uint ]
```

The COVERAGE of a `sealed_members` frame at ordinal `o` is the ordinals from `first` to `o - 1`. Its entries list every `chunk`
and `blob` member of every sealed pack under the SAME key index whose ordinal lies in the coverage: the member's unit digest, the
pack's ordinal and the member's zero-based index in the pack's directory, sorted ascending bytewise on `E(unit_digest)` (the
bytes compared as unsigned integers from the first byte, a proper prefix before the longer string), then ascending on `ordinal`,
then ascending on `member_index`, the integers compared as numbers. When `first` is above `0`, the frame at ordinal `first` is the PREVIOUS member index: a sealed frame under
the same key index whose inner type is `sealed_members`, whose coverage ends at `first - 1`. The index of a key is so a chain from
the last `sealed_members` frame back to one whose `first` is `0`; the ordinal `0` is never a sealed frame, because a `keys` frame
stands before every sealed frame (`P8-27`).

A member entry is a CLAIM, as the clear index is: a unit is accepted only when its bytes match its digest, so a false entry
can hide a unit or name a wrong frame and never yields wrong content; and a digest the index does not list is not therefore
absent.

1. `P8-43` `entry_count` MUST equal the length of the payload list, `first` MUST be smaller than the frame's own ordinal, and
    the entries MUST be in the stated order with no repetition; a violation is `malformed_value` with reason
    `inconsistent_counts`, `out_of_range` (a `first` that names the frame itself or a later frame) or `unordered_or_repeated`.
2. `P8-44` A reader that opens every sealed frame of a key index MUST compare that key's chain of member indexes with the packs
    it opened: one `index_claim_failed` with reason `sealed_members`, at the position of the member index whose claim fails, for each entry that its pack does not bear
    out, for each member of a pack in a coverage that no entry lists, and for each `first` above `0` that does not name a valid
    previous member index. A reader that follows a chain of member indexes MUST require strictly decreasing ordinals. It MUST NOT
    report a unit as missing on the index alone.
3. `P8-45` A writer that seals at least one pack under a key index MUST write the `sealed_members` frames of that key index as
    follows, and no other `sealed_members` frame under it. The sealed packs under the key index, in ascending ordinal, are cut into
    consecutive RUNS: each run is the longest run of consecutive packs, beginning at the first pack that no earlier run holds,
    whose `chunk` and `blob` members together number at most `Nr` (`1048576`, the count constant of the entry frames of `P7`,
    "The constants"); a pack that alone holds more members than that forms a run by itself. One `sealed_members` frame covers each
    run. It stands immediately after the last sealed pack of its run, so that its coverage ends at the ordinal just before its own,
    and its `first` is the ordinal of the previous `sealed_members` frame under the same key index, or `0` when there is none. A key
    index has one `sealed_members` frame when the members of all its packs number at most `Nr`. The value it brings: a reader that looks up one unit opens the chain of member indexes
    and the frames no coverage includes, instead of every sealed pack of the key.
4. `P8-62` A reader that opens a `sealed_members` frame MUST judge its place and cut, after the rules of `P8-43` and before it
    compares the claims of the frame (`P8-44`), and MUST report a frame that breaks them as `malformed_value` with the reason
    `unordered_or_repeated`. A `sealed_members` frame BREAKS THE LAYOUT when its coverage holds no sealed pack under its key index;
    or the ordinary frame before it is not a sealed pack under its key index (judged on the envelope for the key index and on the
    inner type of a frame that was opened; a frame that was not opened to an inner frame counts as a pack); or its coverage holds
    more than `Nr` members of more than one pack; or the next sealed pack under its key index after the frame, with no member
    index of that key index between them, has members that together with those of the coverage number at most `Nr`. The frame is then opaque with `malformed`, lists no entry and is no
    member index for `P8-44`. A reader that opens every sealed frame of a key index MUST also form the runs of `P8-45` from the
    packs it opened, and MUST report, for each run whose last pack is not directly followed by an ordinary frame that is a
    `sealed_members` frame under the same key index, one `index_claim_failed` with the reason `sealed_members`, at the position of
    that last pack: an absent claim is a failed claim.

A reader that looks up one unit under a key opens the sealed frames of that key from the end of the segment backwards until one is
a `sealed_members` frame, follows `first` from there opening member indexes only, and then also opens, in ascending ordinal, every sealed
frame of the key that no coverage of that chain includes and that the backward pass has not already opened. A frame that the
chain names by a `first` is opened as a part of the chain even when it then proves to be no member index (`P8-62`); the chain
ends there, and that frame is not opened again. The frames from the end of the segment backwards are opened in
descending ordinal. A lookup is a use of the format, not a different reading: the reading of 7.2 opens every
frame.

## 9. The key wrap

### 9.1 The suite

The one key-wrap suite, `hpke_base_xwing`, transports a segment key to a recipient by HPKE (RFC 9180) in base mode with the
single-shot interface, with every choice fixed:

- mode: `mode_base`, `0x00`; `psk` and `psk_id` empty (RFC 9180, sections 5 and 5.1.1)
- KEM: X-Wing, `kem_id` `0x647A`, exactly as `draft-connolly-cfrg-xwing-kem-11` defines it; `Nsecret` 32, `Nenc` 1,120, `Npk`
  1,216, `Nsk` 32
- KDF: HKDF-SHA256, `kdf_id` `0x0001`, `Nh` 32
- AEAD: AES-256-GCM, `aead_id` `0x0002`, `Nk` 32, `Nn` 12, `Nt` 16
- `suite_id`: the ten bytes `48 50 4b 45 64 7a 00 01 00 02` (`HPKE` and the three identifiers, two bytes each, big-endian)
- `aad`: the empty string
- `pt`: `SK`, 32 bytes, so `wrapped` is 48 bytes

The two bytes `64 7a` are a constant of `suite_id`; the KEM is the construction of that draft revision, whatever a registry cites
later. A recipient's public key is the 1,216-byte X-Wing encapsulation key; its decapsulation key is held as the 32-byte X-Wing
seed, from which the encapsulation key is derived by that draft's key generation. The HPKE function `DeriveKeyPair` is not used.
The X25519 output inside X-Wing is used as that draft computes it, with no further check.

### 9.2 The recipient entry

```text
recipient_entry = [ suite: enum(key_wrap_suite), recipient_key_id: digest, target_key_id: bytes32,
                    encapsulation: bytes, wrapped: bytes ]
wrap_info       = [ header_identifier: digest, suite: enum(key_wrap_suite), recipient_key_id: digest, target_key_id: bytes32 ]
```

`recipient_key_id` is `H_ctx(recipient_key_id, E([suite, public_key]))` of the recipient's public key; `target_key_id` names the
key entry whose segment key is wrapped; `encapsulation` is HPKE's `enc` and `wrapped` its ciphertext. The HPKE `info` is
`E(wrap_info)`, 104 bytes, with `header_identifier` the segment's `h0`. It binds the segment, the suite, the recipient's key
identifier and the target key; `suite_id` binds the KEM, KDF and AEAD.

WRAP, by the writer: `(encapsulation, wrapped) = SealBase(pkR, info = E(wrap_info), aad = "", pt = SK)`, with `pkR` the
recipient's public key and the encapsulation randomness drawn from a cryptographic random generator.

UNWRAP, by a reader that holds the recipient key: `SK' = OpenBase(encapsulation, skR, info = E(wrap_info), aad = "", wrapped)`,
with `skR` derived from the held seed. A decapsulation error or a refusal of the AEAD is a failure of the entry.

The key schedule of HPKE base mode, written out from RFC 9180 sections 4 and 5.1 so that it can be built from HKDF:
`LabeledExtract(salt, label, ikm) = HKDF-Extract(salt, "HPKE-v1" || suite_id || label || ikm)`;
`LabeledExpand(prk, label, x, L) = HKDF-Expand(prk, I2OSP(L, 2) || "HPKE-v1" || suite_id || label || x, L)`; with
`(shared_secret, enc) = Encap(pkR)`: `psk_id_hash = LabeledExtract("", "psk_id_hash", "")`,
`info_hash = LabeledExtract("", "info_hash", info)`, `context = 0x00 || psk_id_hash || info_hash`,
`secret = LabeledExtract(shared_secret, "secret", "")`, `key = LabeledExpand(secret, "key", context, 32)`,
`base_nonce = LabeledExpand(secret, "base_nonce", context, 12)`, and `wrapped = AES-256-GCM.Seal(key, base_nonce, "", SK)`. The
labels are those of RFC 9180 and are not values of this format. The empty salt of `LabeledExtract` is an HMAC key of length `0`:
HPKE's internal keys are as RFC 9180 fixes them and lie outside `P8-4` and Theorem C.

1. `P8-46` A writer MUST wrap and a reader MUST unwrap exactly as above; a reader MUST NOT apply any other key schedule, KEM, KDF
    or AEAD to a recipient entry.
2. `P8-47` A writer MUST draw the encapsulation randomness of every wrap from a cryptographic random generator, MUST NOT derive
    it from any secret or from the wrapped key, and MUST NOT reuse it.
3. `P8-60` A recipient public key that a writer is given MUST be `bytes` of exactly 1,216 bytes; a key of another length is an
    error of the wrap call. Any check of the key that the primitive makes is the primitive's own, internal to `Encap` and to
    `Decap`: its failure is an error of the wrap call or a failure of the entry (9.2), and no finding depends on it. The format
    gives no meaning to any part of `encapsulation`, `wrapped` or a public key; the primitive of the one document of 9.1 splits them.

### 9.3 Rules of recipient entries

The rules of this subsection cost the one entry that breaks them: the entry is not tried, and the rest of its `keys` frame is
applied. The finding is one for each such entry, at the `keys` frame, and it does not make the frame opaque. The rules are judged
on an entry in this order: `P8-48`; the first clause of `P8-49` (the target); the second clause of `P8-49` (the first use);
`P8-57` (the order within the list, which also refuses a repeated pair). The first that the entry breaks gives its one finding
and its reason. The entries that have one target stand in one frame, the frame of that key (`P8-58`, `P8-49`), so they stand in
this ORDER OF ENTRIES: by their index in the list of that frame. An entry is ADMITTED when its frame is applied (`P8-13`) and it
breaks none of the rules of this subsection; an entry that breaks any rule is not tried (9.4).

1. `P8-48` `encapsulation` MUST be exactly 1,120 bytes and `wrapped` exactly 48 bytes; another length is `malformed_value` with
    reason `wrong_length` for the entry.
2. `P8-49` `target_key_id` MUST be the `key_id` of an entry of the key table after the entries of the same frame are added;
    another value is `malformed_value` with reason `out_of_range` for the entry. A recipient entry whose target key entry already
    has a sealed frame under it earlier in the segment is `malformed_value` with reason `unordered_or_repeated` for the entry: the
    recipient entries of a key are final before its first use. First use is judged on the envelope: any frame of type `sealed`
    earlier in the segment whose `key_index` names the target, whatever its link, payload or outcome; the comparison is of
    key indexes, so a frame that named the index before the entry existed counts.
3. `P8-50` Two recipient entries of one segment with equal `recipient_key_id` and equal `target_key_id` MUST NOT exist. A repeat
    within one frame is refused by `P8-57`, and a repeat in another frame by `P8-49`, since its target has a sealed frame under it;
    no further rule judges it.

### 9.4 Resolving the segment key of a key entry

The resolution runs on a valid `sealing_input` (4.4): an input that is not valid was refused before the file was read, so that in
`segment_keys` at most one entry lists a `key_id`, and in `recipient_keys` at most one entry lists a `suite` with a
`recipient_key_id`, whose seed derives that identifier. Before it opens the first sealed frame under a key entry, a reader resolves
that entry's segment key, once for the read:

1. CANDIDATES. If `segment_keys` lists the entry's `key_id`, that key is a candidate. For every recipient entry of the segment
   whose target is this entry, that `9.3` admits, and for which the reader holds a recipient key, taken in the ORDER OF ENTRIES
   (9.3), the reader unwraps it with the seed of that one recipient key; each success is a candidate, and each failure is one `unwrap_failed`, reported at the first
   sealed frame under the entry.
2. CONFLICT. If two candidates differ, the entry has no segment key: `key_conflict`, reported at the first sealed frame under the
   entry; every sealed frame under it is opaque with reason `missing_key`.
3. NONE. If there is no candidate, the entry has no segment key: one `segment_key_not_held`, at the first sealed frame under the
   entry, with `count` the number of sealed frames under it in the segment; every such frame is opaque with reason `missing_key`.
4. Otherwise the one candidate is the entry's segment key.

The recipient entries of a key are final before its first use (`P8-49`), so the resolution never changes after a frame under the
key was opened, and a streaming reader never waits.

1. `P8-51` A reader MUST resolve the segment key of a key entry by 9.4 before it opens any sealed frame under it, and MUST try
    every admitted recipient entry for which it holds a recipient key.
2. `P8-52` A recipient MUST hold its decapsulation key in the 32-byte seed form of 9.1. (The X-Wing draft states, in its section
    6.1, that X-Wing has the binding properties it names `MAL-BIND-K-PK` and `MAL-BIND-K-CT`, with the reference to a proof still
    to be supplied, and, in its section 5.5.1, that they do not hold when the regular ML-KEM decapsulation key is transmitted
    between implementations. Neither property is part of assumption `S-4`, and no proof of this part uses them.)

A recipient entry gives confidentiality of `SK` toward everyone but the recipient. It authenticates no sender: anyone who holds
the recipient's public key can write one (15, item 4). It does not commit to the key: two recipients can be given entries that
unwrap to different keys, and what protects a frame then is the conflict rule for one reader and Theorem C for two readers (12.4).

## 10. What a party without keys can do, and what leaks

### 10.1 What it can do

A party that holds no key (a relay, a mirror, a verifier of structure) can, with no key and no codec: delimit every item; compute
and check every identifier, the link of every frame, every commit and every signature; check every sealed frame's ciphertext
against its `decoded_length` and `payload_digest` (7.2 step 1), so that it finds accidental damage; check the clear content of
every sealed frame (7.2 step 2), the ordinal included, so that it finds a sealed frame moved within its segment; read the key
table and the recipient entries; count sealed frames per key index; and carry any segment verbatim. Nothing in this part requires
a key to carry a segment or to verify its chain.

It cannot: open a sealed frame, check `sv` or the tag, learn an inner type, content, length before padding or digest, fold the
segment, compute its content digest, or resolve a binding, a suppression entry or a recipe entry that names sealed content.

### 10.2 What leaks, by design

Every clear value of a sealed frame and of a `keys` frame is in one of these classes, and nothing else is in the clear:

- that a frame is sealed, its position and the order of all frames. from: the envelope; how much: exactly.
- how many sealed frames each key index seals, and which key index seals each. from: `key_index`; how much: exactly.
- the padded length of each sealed plaintext. from: `decoded_length`, `len(C) - 16`; how much: `padme` keeps the leading `s` bits
  of the length; at most about nine bits below `2^27` (6.5).
- equality of two sealings of one `(SK, AD, P)`. from: equal `sv` and equal `C`; how much: only between frames with equal `AD`, so
  at one place of one segment of one key context: two renderings of one file under one key and one context show which frames are
  unchanged.
- the key identifiers, key contexts and recipient key identifiers, and how many recipients each key has. from: the `keys` frame;
  how much: exactly; a recipient key identifier names a public key.
- that two files share a segment key. from: equal `key_id`; how much: exactly, when the writer reuses a key identifier.

The compression channel. A sealed plaintext is compressed before padding; an observer who controls part of a plaintext and sees
padded lengths learns about the rest of it. Padding narrows the channel to the leading bits of the compressed length and does not
close it. The writer's defence is `sealed_storage` `stored_plain` for a frame that mixes a secret with data an adversary
influences.

Equality. Deterministic encryption shows equality of identical inputs, and Theorem E's ideal world shows it too (11.3): it is part
of the claim, not a gap in it. Because `AD` holds `h0`, the key context, the key index and the ordinal, equal ciphertexts arise
only for the same frame at the same place under the same key, the same segment header and the same context.

1. `P8-53` A reader MUST NOT derive any value of a sealed frame's inner frame from its clear fields; the only clear values of a
    sealed frame are those of 6.1.

## 11. Security definitions

### 11.1 The scheme as the theorems see it

`𝒜` is the set of the encodings `E(seal_ad)` of all values of `seal_ad` (6.2). It is PREFIX-FREE: no element of `𝒜` is a proper
prefix of another element of `𝒜`, because each is one definite-length CBOR item, which determines its own end; so if
`AD_1 || X_1 = AD_2 || X_2` with `AD_1` and `AD_2` in `𝒜`, then `AD_1 = AD_2` and `X_1 = X_2`. For `SK` of 32 bytes, `AD` in `𝒜`
and a byte string `P` with `1 <= len(P) <= 2^27`, the construction of sections 5 and 6 defines:

```text
Seal(SK, AD, P):        (K_aead, K_commit) = KS(SK);  sv = HMAC(K_commit, AD || P);
                        C = GCMSIV.Enc(K_aead, sv[0..12], AD, P);  return (sv, C)
Open(SK, AD, sv, C):    (K_aead, K_commit) = KS(SK);  P = GCMSIV.Dec(K_aead, sv[0..12], AD, C)
                        if P is a refusal: return fail_aead
                        if HMAC(K_commit, AD || P) != sv: return fail_commit
                        return P
```

`KS` is the key schedule of section 5 with the one cipher of version `2`. `Open` is steps 5 to 7 of 7.2; the steps before them are
public tests on the envelope (a length of `sv` other than 32 bytes, a `C` shorter than 17 bytes or longer than `2^27 + 16`), and
the steps after them examine `P` only after both checks passed. `P` here is the padded plaintext: the theorems are about the
sealed bytes, and what they decode to is the subject of the inner record's own checks (7.2 steps 8 and 9).

### 11.2 Commitment

Game `CMT`. The adversary outputs `sv`, `C` and two tuples `T_1 = (SK_1, AD_1, P_1)` and `T_2 = (SK_2, AD_2, P_2)`, with each
`SK_i` of 32 bytes and each `AD_i` in `𝒜`. It wins if `T_1 != T_2` and `Open(SK_i, AD_i, sv, C) = P_i` for `i = 1` and `i = 2`.
Its advantage `Adv_cmt(A)` is the probability that it wins. Nothing is secret and nothing is random: the adversary chooses both
keys. This game is in the standard model; `GCMSIV` is the real AES-256-GCM-SIV.

This is the decryption-based context-commitment notion `CMTD-4` of Bellare and Hoang ("Efficient Schemes for Committing
Authenticated Encryption", EUROCRYPT 2022, Figure 4; a revised version is Cryptology ePrint Archive, Report 2022/268) with the
nonce removed, because here the nonce is a function of the commitment. It covers commitment to the key alone (`SK_1 != SK_2` with
one `AD`), to the context alone (two `AD` under one key) and to both. Winning needs only steps 5 to 7 to accept twice, which is
easier than a full opening, so the claim covers full openings. Within one file a reader derives one `AD` for a frame (6.2, and a
repeated `key_id` is refused, `P8-14`), so the double openings that a file allows differ in `SK` alone: two readers holding
different keys for one `key_id`, or recipient entries that give two recipients two keys. The game allows more and costs nothing.

### 11.3 Multi-key deterministic authenticated encryption, with the leakage of equality

THE MODEL. The games of this subsection, of Theorem E, of Corollaries W and F, and of the cipher's assumption `S-3` are relative
to an IDEAL CIPHER `π: {0,1}^256 x {0,1}^128 -> {0,1}^128`, a uniformly random permutation of 128-bit blocks for each 256-bit key,
which implements AES-256 inside AES-256-GCM-SIV. The adversary has the oracles `π` and `π^-1` and makes at most `p` queries to
them in all. Every other primitive (SHA-256, HMAC, HKDF) is the real function.

Game `DAE`. There are `u` keys `SK_1` to `SK_u`, each 32 bytes, uniform and independent. The adversary has two oracles besides `π`
and `π^-1`, and makes at most `q_{e,i}` encryption queries under key `i` (`q_e` their sum) and `q_d` decryption queries in all. In
every query `AD` is in `𝒜`, `1 <= len(P) <= 2^27`, `sv` has 32 bytes and `17 <= len(C) <= 2^27 + 16`.

- REAL. `Enc(i, AD, P)` returns `Seal(SK_i, AD, P)`. `Dec(i, AD, sv, C)` returns `Open(SK_i, AD, sv, C)`: a plaintext, `fail_aead`
  or `fail_commit`. `GCMSIV` evaluates AES-256 through `π`.
- IDEAL. `Enc(i, AD, P)` returns, for each new triple `(i, AD, P)`, a uniform 32-byte string `sv` and a uniform string `C` of
  `len(P) + 16` bytes, and the same answer when the triple is repeated. `Dec(i, AD, sv, C)` returns `fail_commit` if an earlier
  `Enc(i, AD, P')` returned `(sv_0, C)` with `sv_0[0..12] = sv[0..12]`, and `fail_aead` otherwise; it never returns a plaintext.
  The oracles `π` and `π^-1` are present and independent of these answers.
- The adversary does not ask `Dec(i, AD, sv, C)` when an earlier `Enc(i, AD, P)` returned `(sv, C)`.

`Adv_dae(A) = Pr[A outputs 1 in REAL] - Pr[A outputs 1 in IDEAL]`.

This is deterministic authenticated encryption (Rogaway and Shrimpton, "A Provable-Security Treatment of the Key-Wrap Problem",
EUROCRYPT 2006, whose full version of 2007 is titled "Deterministic Authenticated-Encryption", Definition 1) in the multi-user
form of Bellare and Tackmann ("The Multi-User Security of Authenticated Encryption: AES-GCM in TLS 1.3", CRYPTO 2016) and of Bose,
Hoang and Tessaro (EUROCRYPT 2018), with TWO failure symbols. The ideal world repeats its answer to a repeated query: equality of
identical inputs is the leakage the notion allows. The ideal decryption oracle gives exactly the information a reader gives by its
two reasons (7.3): whether `(sv[0..12], AD, C)` is a ciphertext that encryption produced. With one failure symbol the notion would
be false for the reader as specified: a keyless adversary asks `Enc(i, AD, P)`, changes one bit of `sv` after byte 12 and asks
`Dec`; the real answer is `fail_commit` and the ideal one would be the single failure.

### 11.4 What the key wrap must provide

The wrap is a labelled public-key encryption scheme: `Wrap(pkR, L, SK)` with label `L = E(wrap_info)`, and
`Unwrap(skR, L, (enc, w))`. Game `LCCA`. There are `r` recipient key pairs, generated honestly. The adversary receives the public
keys and has two oracles: `Chal(j, L, m_0, m_1)` with `len(m_0) = len(m_1) = 32` returns `Wrap(pk_j, L, m_b)` for a hidden bit
`b`; `Unw(j, L, x)` returns `Unwrap(sk_j, L, x)`, except for a triple `(j, L, x)` such that `Chal(j, L, ...)` returned `x`. It
outputs a guess of `b`. `Adv_lcca(A; r, q_c, q_u)` is twice its advantage over one half, with `q_c` challenge and `q_u` unwrap
queries.

The wrap must provide this notion and nothing else: confidentiality of a wrapped key toward everyone but its recipient, under any
label, while the adversary may have other entries unwrapped. It need not authenticate a sender, and need not commit to the key
(9.4, 12.4).

### 11.5 One-time authenticated encryption under many keys

Game `AE1` for AES-256-GCM. There are `n` keys `k_1` to `k_n`, each 32 bytes, uniform and independent, and `n` nonces `N_1` to
`N_n` of 12 bytes, chosen by the adversary before the keys are drawn. The adversary asks `Enc(l, m)` at most once for each `l`,
and `Dec(l, c)` for any `c` other than the answer of `Enc(l, ...)`.

- REAL. `Enc(l, m)` returns `GCM.Enc(k_l, N_l, "", m)`; `Dec(l, c)` returns `GCM.Dec(k_l, N_l, "", c)`, a plaintext or a refusal.
- IDEAL. `Enc(l, m)` returns a uniform string of `len(m) + 16` bytes; `Dec(l, c)` returns a refusal.

`Adv_ae1(A; n, q) = Pr[A outputs 1 in REAL] - Pr[A outputs 1 in IDEAL]`, with `q` queries to `Dec`. A key with no `Enc` query is
allowed: then every `Dec` under it is a forgery attempt against a key that encrypted nothing.

## 12. Theorem C: commitment

### 12.1 Statement

**Theorem C.** There is an algorithm `X` that, given any winning output of the game `CMT` (11.2), evaluates SHA-256 at most twelve
times, each on an input of at most `2^27 + 217` bytes, and outputs two different byte strings with the same SHA-256 digest. Hence,
for every adversary `A`, `Adv_cmt(A) <= Adv_cr(SHA-256; X∘A)`, the probability that `X` run on `A`'s output yields a collision of
SHA-256, and `X∘A` costs the time of `A` and twelve evaluations of SHA-256.

The theorem is in the standard model and assumes of SHA-256 only that collisions are hard to find (assumption `S-0`). It uses
nothing of AES-256-GCM-SIV, of BLAKE3, of the secrecy or randomness of any key, of any oracle or of any idealised primitive. Its
generic classical level is the birthday bound of SHA-256, `2^128` evaluations; it states no level against a quantum adversary.

### 12.2 Lemma H: HMAC is collision resistant over pairs

Let `H` be a hash function with block length `b` bytes, and let every key have one fixed length `l` with `l <= b`. Write `K'` for
`K` followed by `0^(b - l)`, `ipad` and `opad` for the bytes `0x36` and `0x5c` each repeated `b` times (RFC 2104, section 2),
`w(K, M) = (K' xor ipad) || M` and `z(K, M) = (K' xor opad) || H(w(K, M))`, so that `HMAC(K, M) = H(z(K, M))`.

**Lemma H.** From two pairs `(K_1, M_1) != (K_2, M_2)` with keys of length `l` and `HMAC(K_1, M_1) = HMAC(K_2, M_2)`, four
evaluations of `H` give two different strings with one digest under `H`.

Proof. Compute `w_i`, `H(w_i)` and `z_i` for both pairs.

- If `K_1 != K_2`: padding with zero bytes to length `b` is injective on strings of the one length `l`, so `K'_1 != K'_2`, and
  `z_1` and `z_2` differ in their first `b` bytes. `H(z_1) = H(z_2)` is a collision (outer call).
- If `K_1 = K_2`: then `M_1 != M_2`, so `w_1 != w_2` (equal first `b` bytes, different remainders). If `H(w_1) = H(w_2)`, that is
  a collision (inner call). Otherwise `z_1 != z_2` (equal first `b` bytes, different last bytes) and `H(z_1) = H(z_2)` is a
  collision (outer call). The cases are exhaustive. Nothing about the inside of `H` is used. ∎

Each hypothesis is needed, and the lemma is false without it. With keys of two lengths, `K` and `K || 0x00` have one `K'` and give
one HMAC value with no collision of `H`. A key longer than the block is replaced by its digest (RFC 2104, section 2), so a 65-byte
key and its 32-byte SHA-256 digest give one HMAC-SHA-256 value, and the second is a legal 32-byte key. Equal prefixes of two HMAC
values give only equal prefixes of two digests. This part fixes every HMAC key of sections 5 and 6 at 32 bytes (`P8-4`), below the
64-byte block of SHA-256, and compares `sv` whole (`P8-24`).

### 12.3 Proof of Theorem C

Let `A` output `sv`, `C`, `T_1 != T_2` that win. For `i = 1, 2` let `PRK_i = HMAC(0^32, SK_i)`,
`K_i = HMAC(PRK_i, info(commit) || 0x01)` (the commitment key, section 5) and `m_i = AD_i || P_i`. `X` computes these six HMAC
values and proceeds:

1. Both openings passed step 7, which compares all 32 bytes of `sv`: `HMAC(K_1, m_1) = sv = HMAC(K_2, m_2)`.
2. EVENT F: `(K_1, m_1) != (K_2, m_2)`. Two different pairs with 32-byte keys and one HMAC-SHA-256 output: Lemma H gives a
   collision of SHA-256.
3. Otherwise `K_1 = K_2` and `m_1 = m_2`. Since `AD_1` and `AD_2` are in the prefix-free set `𝒜` and `AD_1 || P_1 = AD_2 || P_2`,
   `AD_1 = AD_2`, and then `P_1 = P_2`. So `T_1 != T_2` leaves `SK_1 != SK_2`: EVENT S.
4. Event S, with equal commitment keys. The two HMAC messages are the same string `info(commit) || 0x01`. If `PRK_1 != PRK_2`, the
   pairs `(PRK_1, info(commit) || 0x01)` and `(PRK_2, info(commit) || 0x01)` differ, have 32-byte keys and one output: Lemma H
   gives a collision of SHA-256. If `PRK_1 = PRK_2`, the pairs `(0^32, SK_1)` and `(0^32, SK_2)` differ, have the one 32-byte key
   `0^32` and one output: Lemma H gives a collision of SHA-256 (its equal-key case).

Every winning output thus yields a collision of SHA-256, with at most six HMAC evaluations by `X`, each two evaluations of
SHA-256, and the evaluations of Lemma H among them. ∎

Remarks. (a) The ciphertext `C` is not used, so the commitment role of `sv` takes nothing from its nonce role. (b) The key
schedule appears in step 4 only through Lemma H: the extract step has the salt as its HMAC key and `SK` as its message, so one key
length holds there whatever `SK` is. (c) A later registry version that adds a sealing cipher puts its value in `info` (section 5);
two tuples under two ciphers then have different HMAC messages in step 4, and the same argument applies, provided the new cipher
keeps this schedule and this synthetic value.

### 12.4 What Theorem C gives a reader

A sealed frame opens, up to a collision of SHA-256, under at most one `(SK, AD)` and to at most one `P`. So two readers that hold
different segment keys for one key entry, or two recipients whose entries unwrap to different keys, can never both accept one
sealed frame: one of them reports `seal_failed`. A reader that gets two keys for one entry opens nothing under it (9.4). What `P`
decodes to is fixed by `P` itself and the inner record's own checks; Theorem C commits to the sealed bytes `P`, padding included.

## 13. Theorem E: multi-key deterministic authenticated encryption

### 13.1 Statement

**Theorem E.** In the ideal-cipher model of 11.3, let `A` be an adversary in the game `DAE` with `u` keys, `q_{e,i}` encryption
queries under key `i` (`q_e` in all), `q_d` decryption queries and `p` queries to the ideal cipher. There are adversaries `A_1`,
`A_2` and `B'`, each running in about the time of `A`, such that

```text
Adv_dae(A) <= u * eps_ks(A_1) + Adv_prf^mu(HMAC; A_2, u, q_e + q_d) + Adv_mrae^mu(AES-256-GCM-SIV; B')
              + sum_i q_{e,i}^3 / (6 * 2^192) + q_d / 2^256 + q_e * q_d / 2^136
```

with every term defined here:

- `eps_ks(A_1)`: the advantage of `A_1` in telling `(K_aead, K_commit) = KS(SK)`, for one uniform 32-byte `SK`, from two uniform
  and independent 32-byte keys (assumption `S-1`). The factor `u` is a hybrid over the keys.
- `Adv_prf^mu(HMAC; A_2, u, q)`: the advantage of `A_2` in telling `u` instances of `HMAC(K_j, .)` under independent uniform
  32-byte keys from `u` independent random functions with 32-byte outputs, with `q` queries in all (assumption `S-2`). By a hybrid
  over the instances it is at most `u` times the single-instance advantage.
- `Adv_mrae^mu(AES-256-GCM-SIV; B')`: the advantage of `B'` in the multi-user misuse-resistant AE game whose verification oracle
  returns one bit and whose adversary has the ideal-cipher oracle `Prim` (Bose, Hoang and Tessaro, Figure 1; assumption `S-3`).
  `B'` has `u` users; makes at most `p` queries to `Prim`, forwarding those of `A`; makes at most `q_e` encryption and `q_d`
  verification queries; memoises its verification queries (13.2), so that it never repeats a query, and never verifies an answer of
  its own encryption; makes at most TWO encryption
  queries per (user, nonce), each of at most `2^23` blocks of plaintext and ten blocks of associated data, so at most
  `B = 2 * (2^23 + 10)` blocks per (user, nonce); and uses one nonce for at most `d <= u` users.
- `sum_i q_{e,i}^3 / (6 * 2^192)`: the probability of the event `Coll3`, that three encryption queries under one key with
  different `(AD, P)` receive one 96-bit nonce.
- `q_d / 2^256`: the probability that a decryption query names a value of the random function at a point no encryption defined.
- `q_e * q_d / 2^136`: the probability, in the ideal game of the cipher, that an encryption answer equals the ciphertext of a
  verification query that the reduction asked before and that was refused (13.2, the stop of `B`).

The parameters count every use of a key. When the theorem is applied to files, `q_{e,i}` is the number of frames sealed under
`SK_i` by ALL of its holders together (every holder can seal, section 15 item 7; Corollary F takes the writer as the only holder
that seals), and `q_d` the number of openings under it by every reader to which the adversary can submit frames. The per-key
limits of 7.1 are limits on that total: no one writer can enforce them alone, so the numbers of 13.4 hold under the assumption
that the holders of each key together stay within them (`P8-26`).

The bound holds for `|Pr[REAL] - Pr[IDEAL]|`, since an adversary that inverts its output is an adversary of the same cost.

### 13.2 Proof

`Pr[G]` is the probability that `A` outputs 1 in game `G`. In every game `A`'s queries to `π` and `π^-1` are answered by the ideal
cipher, and `GCMSIV` evaluates AES-256 through it.

**`G0`** is REAL.

**`G1`** replaces, for every key `i`, `(K_aead_i, K_commit_i)` by two uniform independent 32-byte keys. Each `SK_i` enters the
game only through `KS` (`P8-18`, and the game gives `SK_i` to no one), so a hybrid over the `u` keys, each step answered by `A_1`
with its challenge in place of one key's pair, the other keys simulated, and the ideal cipher sampled lazily by `A_1` itself
(it is independent of `KS`), gives `Pr[G0] - Pr[G1] <= u * eps_ks`.

**`G2`** replaces each `HMAC(K_commit_i, .)` by an independent random function `rho_i` with 32-byte outputs, sampled lazily. `A_2`
holds every `K_aead_i` itself (they are used for nothing but the cipher, `P8-18`), samples the ideal cipher lazily, and asks its
oracle `i` once for each encryption query and once for each decryption query whose ciphertext the cipher accepts, so at most
`q_e + q_d` times: `Pr[G1] - Pr[G2] <= Adv_prf^mu(HMAC; A_2, u, q_e + q_d)`. In `G2` a decryption query that the cipher accepts
with plaintext `P'` makes a TEST: it compares `sv` with `rho_i(AD || P')`.

**`G2'`** is `G2` except that a test at a point that no encryption query has defined fails, and `rho_i` is still sampled there but
its value is not used. The two games are identical until a test at such a point would pass in `G2`. In `G2'` the value sampled at
such a point is independent of everything `A` has seen when it chose `sv` (no answer depended on it), so each test passes with
probability `2^-256` at most, and `Pr[G2] - Pr[G2'] <= q_d / 2^256`. In `G2'` only encryption queries evaluate `rho_i`, and
`AD || P` is different for different `(AD, P)` (`𝒜` is prefix-free, 11.1), so each new `(i, AD, P)` receives a uniform `sv`
independent of all before it, and so a uniform nonce. Hence
`Pr[Coll3] <= sum_i C(q_{e,i}, 3) * 2^-192 <= sum_i q_{e,i}^3 / (6 * 2^192)`, in `G2'` and in IDEAL alike, where it is the same
event on the uniform strings `sv`.

**The reduction `B`** plays the multi-user game of the cipher with the oracles that game provides: an encryption oracle, a
verification oracle `Vf` that returns a bit, and `Prim`, to which it forwards every query of `A` to `π` or `π^-1`. It samples
every `rho_i` itself, keeps a table of its own encryption queries, and keeps a list `F` of the underlying verification queries to
which `Vf` answered `false`.

- `Enc(i, AD, P)`, new: `sv = rho_i(AD || P)`; it asks its encryption oracle `(i, sv[0..12], AD, P)`, stores
  `(i, sv[0..12], AD, P, C)` and returns `(sv, C)`. A new `(i, AD, P)` is a new query of that oracle, because `AD` and `P` are
  fields of it. A repeated `(i, AD, P)` gets the stored answer and asks nothing.
- `Dec(i, AD, sv, C)`: if the table holds entries `(i, sv[0..12], AD, P_0, C)`, `B` asks nothing; for each such `P_0` the test
  passes only if `Enc(i, AD, P_0)` returned this `sv`, which makes the query one the game forbids; so `B` returns `fail_commit`.
  If the table holds no such entry, let `v = (i, sv[0..12], AD, C)`, the UNDERLYING QUERY of this decryption query: it omits
  `sv[12..32]`. If `v` is in `F`, `B` asks nothing and returns `fail_aead`. Otherwise `B` asks `Vf(v)`; on `false` it adds `v` to
  `F` and returns `fail_aead`; on `true` it stops and outputs 1.
- `B` MEMOISES its underlying queries. Two decryption queries of the game that agree in `(i, AD, sv[0..12], C)` and differ only in
  the tail `sv[12..32]` give one underlying query: the first is asked, and the second is answered `fail_aead` from `F`. Distinct
  decryption queries of `A` need not give distinct underlying queries, but the underlying queries of `B` are pairwise distinct:
  `B` asks `v` only when `v` is not in `F`, every query answered `false` is in `F`, and a query answered `true` ends the run. `B`
  never asks `Vf` on a triple that its table shows as an answer of its own encryption, and never needs a plaintext from an
  oracle. The same pattern is in Bellare and Hoang's proof of their Theorem 7.3. The order of 7.2, the
  cipher first and the synthetic value after, is the order `B` follows, and it must be: with the order reversed there would be no
  plaintext to hash.
- THE ANSWER FROM `F` IS EXACT. `Vf(v)` is a function of `v` alone: in the real game it is whether the decryption of `C` under
  `(K_aead_i, sv[0..12], AD)` is a refusal, which does not change with time, and in the ideal game it is `false` for every query
  that is not forbidden. So the answer `false` that `B` remembers is the answer `Vf` would give again. A later answer of the
  encryption oracle cannot contradict it, for two reasons. First, `B` consults its encryption table before `F`: a decryption query
  whose `(i, sv[0..12], AD, C)` matches an encryption answer given since is answered from the table (`fail_commit`), and `F` is not
  asked. Second, in the real game a ciphertext that encryption returns under `(i, N, AD)` decrypts without refusal (correctness of
  AES-256-GCM-SIV), so `Vf` on it is `true`, and `v` is not in `F`; an encryption answer equal to the ciphertext of an entry of
  `F` therefore has probability `0` there. In the ideal game an encryption answer is a uniform string of at least 17 bytes, equal
  to the ciphertext of one given entry of `F` with probability at most `2^-136`.
- THE STOP OF `B`. The restriction of the imported game on `B'` (Bose, Hoang and Tessaro, Figure 1) forbids `Vf` on a triple
  that an encryption answer produced. It can be read as forbidding only a triple that an EARLIER answer produced, or as
  order-free, so that a query asked before the answer is given counts too. The reduction does not depend on which: `B` stops
  with output `0` whenever an encryption answer equals the ciphertext of an entry of `F` under the same `(i, N, AD)`, so that
  under either reading it has asked no forbidden query. The stop never occurs in the real game. In the ideal game each of at
  most `q_e` answers meets each of at most `q_d` entries with probability at most `2^-136`, so the stop has probability at
  most `q_e * q_d / 2^136`, the last term of 13.1. The term is carried whichever reading is right; it is not needed under the
  first.

With the REAL cipher, `B` answers exactly as `G2'` until `Vf` returns `true`. Take a decryption query `(i, AD, sv, C)` for which
the table holds no entry `(i, sv[0..12], AD, ., C)` and which the cipher accepts, with plaintext `P'` (a query that the cipher
refuses is answered `fail_aead` by `B`, from `F` or by `Vf`, as `G2'` answers it). Either `AD || P'` is not
yet a point of `rho_i`, and the test fails in `G2'`; or `Enc(i, AD, P')` was asked, with answer `(sv'', C'')`. If
`sv''[0..12] = sv[0..12]`, TIDINESS of AES-256-GCM-SIV (section 2: decryption under `(K_aead_i, sv[0..12], AD)` returned `P'` for
`C`, so encryption of `P'` under the same key, nonce and associated data returns `C`) gives `C'' = C`, so the table holds
`(i, sv[0..12], AD, P', C)`, against the case. Otherwise `sv'' != sv`, and since `rho_i(AD || P') = sv''`, the test fails. In
every case `G2'` returns `fail_commit` where `B` stops with output 1. So `B` outputs 1 at least as often as `A` does in `G2'`.
With the IDEAL cipher of that game, `Vf` is never `true` on a non-answer; call `G3` the game `B` then plays. So
`Pr[G2'] - Pr[G3] <= Adv_mrae^mu(B)`.

**`B'`** is `B` stopped with output 0 when `Coll3` occurs. Before `Coll3` each (user, nonce) receives at most two encryption
queries, so `B'` meets the parameters of 13.1. `B` and `B'` differ only at `Coll3`, which has the same probability in `G2'` and in
`B`'s simulation, so `Pr[G2'] <= Pr[B' = 1, real] + Pr[Coll3] <= Adv_mrae^mu(B') + Pr[G3'] + Pr[Coll3]`, where `G3'` is `G3`
stopped with output 0 at `Coll3`.

**`G3'` against IDEAL.** Encryption: before `Coll3`, every new `(i, AD, P)` is a new query of the ideal AE oracle, which answers a
uniform `C` of `len(P) + 16` bytes, and a new point of `rho_i`, which answers a uniform `sv`: the ideal answer; a repeated triple
gets the stored answer in both. Decryption: the ideal AE oracle accepts exactly its own answers, so `G3'` returns `fail_commit`
exactly when an earlier `Enc(i, AD, P_0)` returned `(sv_0, C)` with `sv_0[0..12] = sv[0..12]`, and `fail_aead` otherwise; that is
IDEAL's rule. It never returns a plaintext: an entry `(i, sv[0..12], AD, P_0, C)` passes its test only for the forbidden query,
also when two entries share one `C`. A `C` accepted under another user is no answer under `i` and gives `fail_aead` in both. The
`Prim` answers are those of an ideal cipher independent of the AE answers in both. So `G3'` and IDEAL agree until `Coll3`, where
`G3'` outputs 0, and `Pr[G3'] <= Pr[IDEAL]`.

Adding the hops gives the bound of 13.1. ∎

What the proof does not use: anything of `H_ctx` or BLAKE3; the padding (the game's message is the padded `P`; padding is examined
at step 8 of 7.2, after both checks, so its failure is no oracle for a keyless party); anything of the container. Assumptions
`A-1` to `A-3` enter in Corollary F only.

### 13.3 The cipher's term

Assumption `S-3` takes its numbers from Theorem 6 of Bose, Hoang and Tessaro ("Revisiting AES-GCM-SIV: Multi-user Security, Faster
Key Derivation, and Better Bounds", EUROCRYPT 2018, full version), which states, for AES-GCM-SIV with block length `n` and key
length `k`, the block cipher modelled as an IDEAL CIPHER, POLYVAL `c`-almost-xor-universal and weakly `c`-regular, a fixed
`0 < ε < 1` and `a = ceil(1.5n / ((n - 1)ε)) - 1`, for a `d`-repeating adversary with at most `p` ideal-cipher queries, `q`
encryption and verification queries of total block length `L`, with `p`, `q` and `L` each at most `2^((1 - ε)n - 8)`, and
encryption queries of at most `B` blocks per (user, nonce):

```text
Adv <= 10 / 2^(n/2) + ((17a + 4a^2 + 24d + na) L + (22a + 13d) p) / 2^k
       + (48c + 30) L B / 2^n + ((303 + 108c) L^2 + (192 + 96c) L p) / 2^(n + k)
```

Here `n = 128`, `k = 256`, `c = 1.5` (that paper's Remark 1 for POLYVAL; it requires the input pair to be non-empty, which holds,
since `AD` and `P` are never empty), and this part takes `ε = 1/8`, so `a = 12` and the validity condition is `p, q, L <= 2^104`.
The bound becomes

```text
Adv_mrae^mu(B') <= 2^-60.7 + ((2316 + 24d) L + (264 + 13d) p) / 2^256 + 102 L B / 2^128 + (465 L^2 + 336 L p) / 2^384
```

`L` counts every block of every encryption and verification query of `B'`, so a forgery attempt costs through `L`, linearly: one
verification query of full length adds at most `2^23 + 11` blocks. `B'` memoises (13.2): a decryption query of the game that is
answered from the table or from `F` asks nothing and adds no block, and each underlying query is asked once. So `B'` makes at most
`q_d` verification queries, and `L <= (q_e + q_d)(2^23 + 11)` blocks: memoising never increases the number of
underlying queries or the blocks they count, and the figures of 13.4, which take each decryption query as one underlying query of
full length, remain upper bounds.

### 13.4 Numbers at the per-key limits

At the limits of 7.1, every query of full length (`2^23` blocks of plaintext, ten of associated data, so
`L <= (q_e + q_d)(2^23 + 11)` and `B <= 2^24 + 20`), `d <= u`, and `p <= 2^104` queries to the ideal cipher:

- `102 L B / 2^128`. one key: `q_e = 2^32`, `q_d = 2^10`: `102 * 2^55 * 2^24 / 2^128 = 2^-42.33`; `u` keys, each at that limit:
  `u * 2^-42.33`.
- the other terms of 13.3. one key: `q_e = 2^32`, `q_d = 2^10`: at most `2^-60.68` in all; `u` keys, each at that limit: below
  `2^-60` for every `u` within the validity condition below (their largest parts grow as `24 u L / 2^256` and
  `13 u p / 2^256`).
- `sum_i q_{e,i}^3 / (6 * 2^192)`. one key: `q_e = 2^32`, `q_d = 2^10`: `2^96 / (6 * 2^192) = 2^-98.59`; `u` keys, each at that
  limit: `u * 2^-98.59`.
- `q_d / 2^256`. one key: `q_e = 2^32`, `q_d = 2^10`: `2^-246`; `u` keys, each at that limit: `u * 2^-246`.
- `q_e * q_d / 2^136`. one key: `q_e = 2^32`, `q_d = 2^10`: `2^-94`; `u` keys, each at that limit, with `q_e` and `q_d` their
  totals: `u^2 * 2^-94`.
- one failed opening of full length, through `L`. one key: `q_e = 2^32`, `q_d = 2^10`: `102 * 2^23 * 2^24 / 2^128 = 2^-74.33`; `u`
  keys, each at that limit: the same per opening.

So the statistical and cipher terms are about `2^-42.33` for one key at its limit and `u * 2^-42.33` for `u` keys at theirs. The
bound is below `2^-32` for up to about 1,285 keys, each sealing `2^32` frames of `2^27` bytes, and it is MEANINGFUL (below `1`)
only up to about `2^42.33` keys at the full limit; above that it says nothing. The validity condition `L <= 2^104` holds exactly up
to `562949081007255` keys at the limit (one key at the limit has `L = (2^32 + 2^10)(2^23 + 11) = 36028852853550080`; `2^49` keys
would exceed `2^104`); it is not the binding constraint. The dominant term is proportional to the longest message (`B`) and the
total volume (`L`), so smaller frames give much smaller figures: with every plaintext at most `2^22` bytes (`B` about `2^19`, `L`
about `2^50` per key) one key contributes `2^-52.33`, and `2^20` keys `2^-32.33`. A deployment that seals under many keys keeps
its own count of the total volume, because the term over many keys is governed by the total of blocks and not by the frames of one
key.

The terms `u * eps_ks` and `Adv_prf^mu` have no number from a theorem (assumptions `S-1`, `S-2`). If the best attack on
HMAC-SHA-256 as a pseudo-random function, and on the schedule, is generic (key search, or a collision of 256-bit outputs), each is
at most about `u * (t / 2^256 + (q_e + q_d)^2 / 2^257)` for an adversary of `t` evaluations, which is negligible beside the
cipher's term.

### 13.5 How the per-key limits follow

`max_seals_per_key = 2^32` and `max_sealed_plaintext = 2^27` keep one key's contribution at `2^-42.33` (13.4) and keep `Coll3` at
`2^-98.59`; with them a key seals at most `2^59` bytes. `max_failed_openings = 2^10` keeps the forgery cost of one read at
`2^-64.33` per key, far below the encryption term. `max_key_context = 64` bytes keeps the associated data at ten blocks, the
figure used for `B` and `L`. The limits are for one key and count its TOTAL use by all holders: the holders count the frames
sealed under it together (`P8-26`), and each reader counts failed openings within one read (`P8-37`). No reader can count across
reads or files: a service that opens frames submitted by a party that does not hold the key bounds its failed openings over its
whole life, since `q_d` of the theorem counts every one.

### 13.6 What Theorem E does not cover

- A segment key that is not uniform: a key derived from a password, or from any secret an adversary can guess, is outside the
  theorem, and any one sealed frame lets a holder of the file test a guess offline by the tag alone.
- A segment key that the same file also wraps to a recipient: the recipient entries are further functions of `SK` in the
  adversary's hands. Corollary W (14.2) extends the theorem to such keys.
- A quantum adversary. The reductions are straight-line, so IF the key schedule, HMAC-SHA-256 and AES-256-GCM-SIV keep their
  properties against an adversary whose oracle queries are classical, the bound holds with those advantages. No text cited
  establishes that: the statement used for the cipher is in the ideal-cipher model with classical queries to the cipher, and an
  adversary with a quantum computer evaluates AES offline in superposition. No claim is made against an adversary with
  superposition access to the oracles.

## 14. Wrapped keys, and what a reader of a file gets

THE MODEL OF THIS SECTION. Every game of this section, and every statement that depends on one (Lemma K, Corollary W, and
Corollary F with wrapped keys), is relative to the one ideal cipher `π` of 11.3, which also implements AES-256 inside the wrap's
AES-256-GCM, and to independent random oracles for SHA3-256, SHA3-512 and SHAKE-256, which implement those functions inside X-Wing
(the model of assumption `S-4`). The adversary has access to all of them and makes at most `p` queries to `π` and `h` queries to
the random oracles; every adversary built in a proof forwards to them the queries of the adversary it runs. HMAC, HKDF and
AES-256-GCM do not use SHA3, so no oracle is shared between the KEM and the rest of the wrap.

### 14.1 Lemma K: the wrap from the KEM

The wrap of 9.2, with `L = E(wrap_info)`, is a labelled scheme of the form of 11.4: `(ss, enc) = Encap(pkR)`; `secret` and `(key,
base_nonce)` are derived from `ss` and `L` by the HPKE key schedule (9.2); `w = GCM.Enc(key, base_nonce, "", SK)`. The label
enters the schedule only through `info_hash = HMAC("", "HPKE-v1" || suite_id || "info_hash" || L)`; `secret` is `HMAC(ss,
"HPKE-v1" || suite_id || "secret")`, an HMAC keyed by the 32-byte shared secret; `key` and `base_nonce` are the first output block
of HMAC keyed by `secret` on two different messages that contain `info_hash`.

**Lemma K.** For every adversary `A` of `LCCA` (11.4) against the wrap, with `r` recipients, `q_c` challenge and `q_u` unwrap
queries, `p` queries to `π` and `h` random-oracle queries, there are adversaries `K_0` to `K_5`, each running in about the time of
`A`, such that

```text
Adv_lcca(A; r, q_c, q_u) <= 2 * ( r * q_c * ( Adv_kem(X-Wing; K_0) + 2δ + (q_c + q_u) / 2^256 )
                                  + r * q_c * Adv_kem(X-Wing; K_1) + q_c * δ + Adv_prf^mu(HMAC; K_2, q_c, q_c)
                                  + Adv_prf^mu(HMAC; K_3, q_c, 2(q_c + q_u)) + Adv_cr(SHA-256; K_4)
                                  + Adv_ae1(AES-256-GCM; K_5, q_c + q_u, q_u) )
```

The first line is the probability of the stop event `Coll_enc` of hop 0 of the proof: two honest encapsulations to one recipient, or an
encapsulation that an unwrap query named before it was made, return one `enc`. The terms of the second line and after are those of
the hop 0 (`q_c * δ`) and of the hops 1 to 5. Here `Adv_kem` is the IND-CCA advantage against X-Wing with one public key and one challenge, in the random-oracle model of this
section, of an adversary that makes `h` random-oracle queries and those of the encapsulations and decapsulations it computes
itself (assumption `S-4`), `δ` is the probability, over honest key generation and encapsulation, that decapsulation does not
return the encapsulated shared secret, `Adv_prf^mu` is as in Theorem E (assumption `S-2`), `Adv_cr` is the probability of finding
a collision of SHA-256 (assumption `S-0`), and `Adv_ae1` is the advantage of 11.5 (assumption `S-5`).

Proof. The standard KEM/DEM composition, in a stop event and five hops, from the game with `b = 0` to a game whose view does not
depend on `b`; the same steps lead from `b = 1` to that game, which gives the factor `2`.

The PAIR of a challenge is `(j, enc)`: its recipient and its encapsulation. An unwrap query `(j, L', (enc, w))` has the pair
`(j, enc)`. In the game of 11.4 an unwrap query decapsulates its `enc` under `sk_j`.

0. THE STOP EVENT. Let `G'_b` be the game in which an unwrap query whose pair is that of an EARLIER challenge `c` uses the
   encapsulated shared secret `ss_c` of `c` in place of decapsulation. It differs from the game of 11.4 only when a challenge
   `enc` does not decapsulate to its encapsulated secret, so each `Pr[G'_b]` is within `q_c * δ` of the probability in the game;
   this is the term `q_c * δ` below. Let `Coll_enc` be the event that a challenge to a recipient `j` returns an `enc` that equals
   the `enc` of an earlier challenge to `j`, or the `enc` of an unwrap query for `j` that was asked before that challenge. Every
   game of the hops 1 to 5 is `G'_b` STOPPED with output `0` when `Coll_enc` occurs; stopping changes the probability that `A`
   outputs 1 by at most `Pr[Coll_enc]` in each world. In a stopped game no two challenges have one pair, and no unwrap query
   has, before the challenge, the pair that a challenge later returns. So an unwrap query whose pair is the pair of a challenge
   `c` is asked after `c`, and there is exactly one such `c`: the replacement secret of hop 1 is a function of the pair, drawn
   once, at the challenge, and reused for every unwrap query that has that pair. The event cannot be left out: a repeated pair
   would give two challenges one shared secret and, for one label, one `key` and one `base_nonce`, which the game `AE1` (one
   encryption for each key) excludes. Its probability is bounded from `S-4` by `K_0`, as follows.
   `K_0` draws a bit `b`, an index `j*` from `1` to `r` and an index `t` from `1` to `q_c`; it receives one public key, places it
   as `pk_(j*)`, generates every other key pair itself and plays `G'_b` with `A`, forwarding the random-oracle queries of `A`.
   It encapsulates to every recipient itself and records, for each challenge and for each unwrap query for `j*`, the pair and a
   secret: the encapsulated secret of a challenge, and for an unwrap query for `j*` that is not the pair of an earlier challenge
   the answer of its decapsulation oracle (which, until it has asked for its challenge, answers every query). At the `t`-th
   challenge: if the recipient is not `j*`, `K_0` outputs `0`; otherwise it asks for its single challenge `(ct*, K*)` and outputs
   `1` exactly when `ct*` equals a recorded `enc` for `j*` whose recorded secret is `K*`. If `K*` is the secret encapsulated
   with `ct*`, the output is `1` whenever `Coll_enc` occurs at the `t`-th challenge to `j*`, except when a recorded secret is not
   the decapsulation of its `enc` (only for a challenge that failed to decapsulate, probability at most `δ`) or `K*` is not the
   decapsulation of `ct*` (at most `δ`): at most `2δ` is lost. If `K*` is uniform and independent, `K_0` has asked for it only
   after every recorded value was fixed, so each of at most `q_c + q_u` recorded secrets equals it with probability `2^-256`.
   Hence `K_0` has advantage at least `Pr[Coll_enc at (j*, t)] - 2δ - (q_c + q_u) / 2^256`, averaged over `(b, j*, t)`, and the
   union bound over the `r * q_c` choices of recipient and challenge gives, with the bit `b` averaged,
   `(Pr_0[Coll_enc] + Pr_1[Coll_enc]) / 2 <= r * q_c * ( Adv_kem(X-Wing; K_0) + 2δ + (q_c + q_u) / 2^256 )`. The loss of the
   stop in the two worlds is the sum `Pr_0 + Pr_1`, which the factor `2` of the bound carries. This bound uses `S-4` and the
   correctness of decapsulation only; it takes nothing from the structure of an X-Wing encapsulation, whose randomness would
   give a much smaller figure, which this part does not assume.
1. KEM. Order the challenges by time. The hybrid `H_t`, for `0 <= t <= q_c`, is the stopped game in which each of the first `t`
   challenges uses a uniform 32-byte string `ss*`, drawn at the challenge and stored under its pair, in place of its
   encapsulated shared secret, and each of the others uses its encapsulated secret; an unwrap query whose pair is the pair of an
   earlier challenge is answered with the secret used for that challenge (its stored `ss*` or its encapsulated secret). `H_0` is
   the stopped `G'_b`. To bound `Pr[H_(t-1)] - Pr[H_t]`, `K_1` receives one public key and a decapsulation oracle, and asks for its
   single KEM challenge `(ct*, K*)` when the `t`-th challenge is made; the oracle answers every query before that and, after it,
   every query except `ct*`. Before it hands out the `r` public keys `K_1` draws `j*` uniformly from `1` to `r`, places its key as
   `pk_(j*)` and generates every other key pair itself. For a challenge before the `t`-th it encapsulates to the named recipient
   itself and uses a uniform `ss*`; at the `t`-th, if the named recipient is not `j*`, it stops and outputs `0`, and otherwise
   it uses `(ct*, K*)` as `(enc, ss)`; after the `t`-th it encapsulates itself and uses the secret it encapsulated. An unwrap
   query whose pair is the pair of an earlier challenge gets the secret used for that challenge; any other unwrap query for `j*`
   goes to the decapsulation oracle; for another recipient `K_1` decapsulates itself. `K_1` knows every `enc` that occurs, so it
   stops with output `0` when `Coll_enc` occurs, as the stopped game does. Every random-oracle query of `A` is forwarded. The
   draw of `j*` is independent of `A`'s view up to the `t`-th challenge, and a stop gives `0` whatever `K*` is, so `K_1`'s
   advantage is `(Pr[H_(t-1)] - Pr[H_t]) / r`. With `t` also drawn uniformly by `K_1`, the `q_c` steps sum to at most
   `r * q_c * Adv_kem(X-Wing; K_1)`, and the hop costs `r * q_c * Adv_kem`. In `H_(q_c)` every challenge has its own uniform
   `ss*`, independent of every other, and no two challenges have one pair.
2. SECRET. With each `ss*` uniform, independent and used for nothing else, `secret = HMAC(ss*, constant)` is one query to an HMAC
   instance under a uniform 32-byte key, for each challenge; replacing it, for all challenges at once, by a uniform 32-byte
   string costs `Adv_prf^mu(HMAC; K_2, q_c, q_c)`. Two challenges share no `secret`.
3. LABELS. Two different labels give two different `info_hash` values unless the two HMAC computations with the one empty key
   collide; Lemma H (12.2), whose equal-key case holds for a key of length `0`, turns such a collision into a collision of
   SHA-256: `Adv_cr(SHA-256; K_4)`. So the messages on which one challenge's `secret` is used are different for different labels,
   and different for `key` and `base_nonce`. This is the hop that separates two DEM keys derived from one `secret`: a challenge
   and an unwrap query that pairs its `enc` with another label. Two different challenges share no `secret` and so no DEM key,
   whatever their labels.
4. KEYS. With `secret` uniform, replace `HMAC(secret, .)` by a random function, for all challenges at once; each challenge's
   `secret` is used at most twice for its own label and twice for each other label that an unwrap query pairs with its `enc`, so
   `2(q_c + q_u)` queries in all: `Adv_prf^mu(HMAC; K_3, q_c, 2(q_c + q_u))`. The separation of hop 3 makes these inputs differ
   for different labels. Now each pair (challenge, label) has its own uniform `key` and an independent uniform `base_nonce`, and
   there are at most `q_c + q_u` such pairs, whatever the number of unwrap queries on one of them.
5. AEAD. Each challenge ciphertext is the one encryption under the key of its pair (challenge, own label), and there is one
   challenge for each pair `(j, enc)`, so `Enc` is asked at most once for each key, as `AE1` requires. This is where the stop
   event is used: were the pair `(j, enc)` and the label to recur in two challenges, one `key` and one `base_nonce` would
   encrypt two plaintexts. Every unwrap query on the pair of a challenge `c` is a decryption under the key of its label: for a
   label that is not the challenge's own, under a key that no `Enc` query used, which `AE1` allows; for the challenge's own
   label, with a `w` that differs from the challenge's (the query that the game excludes), and any number of unwrap queries on one
   (challenge, label) are any number of `Dec` queries under one key. `K_5` plays `AE1` with the `q_c + q_u` keys and nonces it
   sampled, asking `Enc` for each challenge and `Dec` for each such unwrap query: `Adv_ae1(AES-256-GCM; K_5, q_c + q_u, q_u)`.
   In the ideal world of `AE1` each challenge ciphertext is a uniform string of 48 bytes and each such unwrap query is refused,
   whatever `b` is. The stop event depends only on the encapsulations and on `A`'s queries, whose distribution in this last game
   does not depend on `b`. ∎

What remains assumed for the wrap is therefore `S-4` (X-Wing, in the random-oracle model), `S-2` (HMAC), `S-0` (SHA-256) and `S-5`
(AES-256-GCM, one encryption per key); the decapsulation failure probability `δ` of X-Wing enters as a term (inside the bracket of the
bound with the factor `2 * r * q_c + q_c`, and so `4 * r * q_c + 2 * q_c` in all), and no number is taken for it here. What is proved is the bound above for the game `LCCA` of 11.4, with
honestly generated recipient keys. What is assumed is `S-0`, `S-2`, `S-4` and `S-5` and the random-oracle model of this section.
OPEN for the cryptographer: that the one-challenge game of `S-4` allows its challenge to be requested at a time that the adversary
chooses, with decapsulation queries answered before it (as the IND-CCA game of a KEM does); `K_0` and `K_1` use that form.

### 14.2 Corollary W: keys wrapped to recipients

**Corollary W.** Extend the game `DAE` to `DAE-W`: before its queries the adversary receives, for each key `i`, recipient entries
`Wrap(pk_j, L, SK_i)` for honest recipient public keys `pk_j` of its choice among `r` and labels `L` of its choice, `n_w` entries
in all, and it has an unwrap oracle that returns `Unwrap(sk_j, L, x)` for any `(j, L, x)` that is not one of those entries. In
REAL-W the entries wrap the segment keys and the oracles are those of REAL; in IDEAL-W each entry wraps an independent uniform
32-byte string and the oracles are those of IDEAL. `Adv_dae-w(A) = Pr[A outputs 1 in REAL-W] - Pr[A outputs 1 in IDEAL-W]`. Then
for every adversary `A` of `DAE-W` there are adversaries `C` and `A'` of about the same cost with the bound below. `C` forwards
the queries of `A` to `π` and to the random oracles; `A'`, which plays `DAE` and simulates the wraps itself, simulates the random
oracles itself (the game `DAE` has none) and makes `p` queries to `π` plus the AES calls of its `n_w + q_u` wraps and unwraps:

```text
Adv_dae-w(A) <= Adv_lcca(C; r, n_w, q_u) + Adv_dae(A')
```

where `Adv_lcca` is the advantage of 11.4 with `n_w` challenge and `q_u` unwrap queries, bounded by Lemma K with `q_c = n_w`, the
term of its stop event `Coll_enc` (repeated encapsulations) included, and `Adv_dae(A')` is bounded by Theorem E. Section 13 does not sum the term: it concerns the game `DAE`,
which has no recipient entries; the term enters a bound for a file only through `Adv_dae-w` here and in Corollary F.

Proof. One hop. `C` samples the `u` segment keys and, for each entry, independent uniform 32-byte strings `R`; for each entry it
asks `Chal(j, L, SK_i, R)` and gives the answer to `A` as the entry; it answers the unwrap oracle with its own `Unw` (a query on a
challenge entry is excluded in both games), and the encryption and decryption oracles with the segment keys it holds. With `b = 0`
this is `DAE-W`; with `b = 1` the entries are independent of the segment keys, and `C`'s simulation of the rest is the game `DAE`
run by an adversary `A'` that samples the `R` strings, the recipient keys' entries and the unwrap answers itself, which it can
because with `b = 1` none of them depends on a segment key. (`A'` holds the recipient key pairs it generated, so it answers unwrap
queries itself.) So `Pr[REAL-W] - Pr[b = 1] <= Adv_lcca(C)`; and `A'`, which wraps fresh strings itself, plays REAL of `DAE` when
the game with `b = 1` is played and IDEAL of `DAE` when IDEAL-W is, so `Pr[b = 1] - Pr[IDEAL-W] <= Adv_dae(A')`. ∎

The unwrap oracle is stronger than what a reader reveals: a reader reports only whether an entry unwrapped and whether two keys
conflict (9.4), and opens frames under an unwrapped key. Corollary W is about confidentiality and integrity toward the holders of
the segment keys; it says nothing about who wrote a recipient entry or a key entry (15, items 4 and 16).

### 14.3 Corollary F: what a reader of a file gets

THE SETTING. An honest writer holds segment keys `SK_1` to `SK_u`, uniform and independent (`P8-7`), named by key entries whose
key identifiers are independent of the keys (`P8-8`); it may also wrap them to honest recipients. An adversary `A` holds no
segment key of the writer and no recipient decapsulation key, may query the ideal cipher `p` times and, when the writer wraps its
keys, the random oracles of this section `h` times, and interacts with the writer and with readers:

- SEALING. `A` names a key `i`, a header identifier `h0`, the key entry `[key_id_i, cipher, ctx]`, a key index `k`, an ordinal `o`
  and an inner record; the writer seals by 6.4 and returns the sealed frame. `A` may also ask, before its other requests (as in
  Corollary W), for recipient entries of the writer's keys to honest recipients, with labels of its choice.
- READING. `A` submits any file `F`. A reader whose `sealing_input` is valid (4.4: an invalid one is refused before the file is
  read, and no statement here concerns it) and lists `SK_i` under `key_id_i` for some keys `i` (and, for
  other key identifiers, only keys that `A` chose), and that holds no recipient key for an entry whose target is a key entry of
  the writer, reads `F` by 7.2; `A` learns every finding of that read, and for each sealed frame whether it was opened or the
  outcome that ended its opening.

The writer is the ONLY holder of `SK_1` to `SK_u` that seals: a recipient that seals under a writer's key is outside the corollary
(15, item 7), since its frames would count as frames the writer never sealed. `q_{e,i}` is the number of frames the writer sealed
under `SK_i`; `q_d` is the number of openings under the writer's keys that reach step 6 of 7.2 in all reads and are not a frame
the writer returned, unchanged, at the place it was sealed for.

**Corollary F.**

1. INTEGRITY. The probability that some reader, at a sealed frame of `F` that stands at ordinal `o` and key index `k` in a segment
   whose header identifier is `h0`, under a key entry `[key_id_i, cipher, ctx]` whose resolved segment key is `SK_i`, passes step
   7 with a plaintext `P` that the writer never sealed under `SK_i` with that `h0`, `key_id_i`, `ctx`, `k` and `o`, is at most
   `Adv_dae(A')` for an adversary `A'` of `DAE` with the same `u`, `q_{e,i}` and `p`, that `q_d`, and the running time of `A` and
   of the reads; it is at most `Adv_dae-w(A')` of Corollary W when the writer also wraps its keys. So a sealed frame moved to
   another ordinal, another key index, another key entry or another key context, or into a segment with another header identifier,
   is not opened, except with that probability; and a reader folds, under a key it holds, only inner frames that the writer sealed
   for that exact place.
2. CONFIDENTIALITY. Let the writer seal, in world `β`, the plaintext `P^β` of each sealing request, where `A` gives pairs
   `(P^0, P^1)` of equal length such that the two sequences of requests repeat their `(i, AD, P)` in the same pattern, and let `A`
   learn from each read only the outcomes of steps 1 to 7 of 7.2 for each sealed frame (not the findings of steps 8 to 10, which
   are functions of the plaintext). Then `A` tells `β = 0` from `β = 1` with advantage at most `2 * Adv_dae(A'')`, or
   `2 * Adv_dae-w(A'')` with wrapped keys, for an adversary `A''` of the same parameters.

Proof. INTEGRITY. `A'` plays `DAE` (or `DAE-W`) and runs `A`, the writer and the readers.

- Sealing. For a request, `A'` computes `P = pad(E(inner))` (6.5) and `AD = E([h0, key_id_i, ctx, k, o])` (6.2), asks
  `Enc(i, AD, P)` and receives `(sv, C)`. Every other field of the writer's frame is a function of `(k, o, sv, C)` and of public
  values: the content `[k, o, sv]`, `decoded_length = len(C)`, `payload_digest = H_ctx(payload, C)` and, by `A-1`, the identifier,
  a function of these fields and of the predecessor. By `A-3` no key is needed to compute them. So `A'` returns exactly the frame
  the writer would. Recipient entries are those the game `DAE-W` provides.
- Reading. By `A-3`, a reader's view of `F` (its segments, items and fields, each frame's ordinal, each segment's `h0` and key
  table) is a deterministic function of `F`, so `A'` runs steps 1 to 5 of 7.2 for every sealed frame without any key of the
  writer. A reader that lists `SK_i` under `key_id_i` and holds no recipient key for an entry whose target is a key entry of the
  writer resolves `SK_i` (9.4); for a key that `A` chose, `A'` holds the key and runs the reader itself. At steps 6 and 7, under a
  writer's key `SK_i`, the reader's associated data is `AD_F = E([h0, key_id_i, ctx, k, o])`, built from `F` alone (`P8-23`). The
  opening is `Open(SK_i, AD_F, sv, C)` by `P8-23` and 7.2; as `A-2` records, `storage`, the stored `payload` beyond the ciphertext
  `C` that the payload check binds (7.2 step 1) and `signatures` enter neither, so nothing else of the frame matters. If an
  earlier `Enc(i, AD_F, P)` returned `(sv, C)`, `A'` knows `P` from its table, and `Open` returns it in REAL by correctness.
  Otherwise `A'` asks `Dec(i, AD_F, sv, C)`, a query the game allows. If it returns a plaintext, the event of the corollary has
  happened, and `A'` outputs 1 and stops; if it returns `fail_aead` or `fail_commit`, that is exactly the reason the reader
  reports (7.3), and `A'` continues. Steps 8 to 10 use only `P`, which `A'` knows for every frame that passed step 7.
- In REAL the simulation is exact until `A'` stops, and `A'` stops only at the event: if `Dec(i, AD_F, sv, C)` returns a plaintext
  `P` and an earlier `Enc(i, AD_F, P)` had returned `(sv_0, C_0)`, then `sv = HMAC(K_commit, AD_F || P) = sv_0` and, by tidiness
  (section 2), `C = C_0`, so the query was answered from the table and not asked. So `A'` outputs 1 with exactly the probability
  of the event. In IDEAL, `Dec` never returns a plaintext, so `A'` outputs 1 with probability `0`. Hence the event has probability
  at most `Adv_dae(A')`.
- `A'` makes `q_{e,i}` encryption queries under key `i`, one for each frame the writer sealed, and `q_d` decryption queries, one
  for each opening that reaches step 6 and is not a writer's frame at its own place.
- The writer sealed `P` with `AD_w = E([h0_w, key_id_i, ctx_w, k_w, o_w])`. The event fails only if `AD_F = AD_w`, and `E` is
  injective on records, so `h0 = h0_w`, `ctx = ctx_w`, `k = k_w` and `o = o_w`: the place in the file is the place the writer
  sealed for. The statement is about `h0` and needs no property of `H_ctx`; reading "equal `h0`" as "equal header" needs that
  `H_ctx` has no collision (`A-1`), and nothing else in the corollary does.

CONFIDENTIALITY. `A''` answers each sealing request with `Enc(i, AD, P^β)` and each read as above, except that it never needs a
plaintext from a read, since `A` learns only the outcomes of steps 1 to 7: the outcome of an unchanged writer frame at its own
place is "opened", and every other opening is a `Dec` query whose answer is the outcome. In IDEAL, the `Enc` answers depend only
on the lengths and on the pattern of repetition, which are the same for `β = 0` and `β = 1`, and the `Dec` answers depend only on
the `Enc` answers; so `A`'s view in IDEAL does not depend on `β`. Two applications of Theorem E (or Corollary W), one for each
`β`, give the bound. ∎

What Corollary F does not say. It covers only a reader that holds the writer's key directly and holds no recipient key for an
entry whose target is a key entry of the writer, and only a writer that is the only holder that seals. A reader whose segment key
comes from a recipient entry is NOT covered: a simulation would have to decide, without the key, whether a key unwrapped from an
entry the adversary wrote equals the writer's, since `key_conflict` reveals exactly that, and no proof of that case is given (15,
item 20; question `Q-9`). Such a reader can also be given a key entry and a recipient entry for a key of the adversary's own, and
then opens the adversary's frames (15, item 4). The corollary says nothing about frames that are missing, about clear frames, or
about frames sealed by another holder of the key (15, items 5 to 7), and its confidentiality holds only toward a party that does
not see a reader's result after opening (15, item 19).

## 15. What is not claimed

Each item states what is not claimed, its consequence, and the remedy, where the container offers one. "An authenticating
signature" is a signature of the container's signature mechanism (outside this document) over the identifier of a frame or of a
commit frame, by a key the reader trusts; the result of reading says which frames such signatures cover.

1. TRAFFIC ANALYSIS. The existence, position, order, key index and padded length of every sealed frame, the number of sealed
   frames per key, and the key table are in the clear (10.2). Nothing hides them.
2. EQUALITY. Identical `(SK, AD, P)` give identical bytes; this is what deterministic authenticated encryption means, and the
   ideal world of Theorem E shows it too. Two renderings of one file under one key and one key context show which frames are
   unchanged.
3. FRESHNESS AND SPLICING. A whole sealed frame can be replaced by another valid sealed frame of the same segment header, key
   entry, key index and ordinal (a frame of an earlier rendering): the cipher accepts it and the chain re-links (`A-3`). Two
   holders that share a segment key and give their files equal headers and equal key contexts make their frames interchangeable in
   the same way. Remedy: an authenticating signature over a later commit, or a trusted head; distinct key contexts (`P8-29`).
4. ORIGIN (no origin integrity from sealing). Anyone who knows a recipient's public key can append, at the end of a segment, a
   `keys` frame with a new key entry and a recipient entry that wraps a key of its own to that recipient, and then sealed frames
   under it; appending at the end moves no ordinal. A reader whose segment key comes from that recipient entry opens and folds
   those frames as it folds the writer's: for such a reader sealing gives confidentiality and no integrity of origin. Remedy: an
   authenticating signature covering those frames; the result of reading says which frames are authenticated.
5. TRUNCATION. Deleting the frames at the end of a segment is not detected by sealing: the ordinal binds a frame's place, not the
   number of frames. Remedy: an authenticating signature over a commit frame, which fixes the frames before it; frames after the
   last signed commit have no such protection.
6. CLEAR FRAMES BESIDE SEALED ONES. A clear frame in a segment that also holds sealed frames is not authenticated by sealing, and
   a key holder folds it beside the inner frames it opened, with nothing in the fold to tell them apart. Remedy: an authenticating
   signature, and the authenticity that the result of reading reports for each frame.
7. EVERY HOLDER CAN SEAL. Every holder of a segment key, every recipient of a wrapped key included, can seal frames that every
   other holder accepts. Opening proves only that some holder of the key sealed the frame for that place. Remedy: an
   authenticating signature by the writer's own key.
8. TOTAL USE OF A KEY. The per-key limits of 7.1 are limits on the TOTAL use of a key by all its holders. One writer cannot
   enforce them alone, so Theorem E's numbers hold only if the holders together stay within them (13.1, `P8-26`). Remedy: a
   segment key for each writer.
9. AGREEMENT BETWEEN RECIPIENTS. Theorem C commits each frame; nothing makes two recipients see one content. A writer can give
   different recipients different key entries, and so different plaintext views of one file; each sees `missing_key` for the
   frames of the others. A commit names a state (`P3`, "Graph states") only to a reader that opened every sealed frame before it
   in its run, so a recipient who lacks the key of one of them finds the state unavailable, and two recipients that each find the
   state find equal states (`P3`, claim ST3, by Theorem C).
10. RECIPIENT AND SIGNER ANONYMITY. A recipient key identifier is a deterministic function of a public key and names it in every
    file, so it links a recipient across files. Signatures are in the clear and name their key; sealing hides no signer.
11. AVAILABILITY. A party that can rewrite the file can deny service: delete frames, give a key entry a conflicting recipient
    entry (so that `key_conflict` stops every frame under it), or put bogus sealed frames before honest ones to use up
    `max_failed_openings`. None of these gives more than deleting frames gives.
12. FORWARD SECRECY AND POST-COMPROMISE SECURITY. Whoever obtains a segment key, or a recipient's decapsulation key, opens every
    frame ever sealed under that segment key, past and future. There is no ratchet and no erasure.
13. WHAT THE PLAINTEXT DECODES TO. The theorems are about the padded plaintext `P`. That the inner payload decodes to its declared
    length and digest is the inner record's own check (7.2 step 9), resting on collision resistance of `H_ctx` as the container
    assumes it for every payload digest.
14. PROTECTION OF THE CLEAR ENVELOPE. The header, the `keys` frame, the clear content of every sealed frame, every clear frame,
    the signatures and the storage fields are readable by every party. The seal binds `h0` and the frame's key and place (6.2); it
    binds nothing else of the clear file, and it never binds `storage`, `payload` beyond the ciphertext, or `signatures`.
15. LOW-ENTROPY KEYS. Nothing is claimed for a segment key derived from a password or another guessable secret.
16. THE WRAP: COMMITMENT AND ORIGIN OF KEYS. Neither HPKE nor the recipient entry commits to the wrapped key; what protects frames
    when recipients are given different keys is Theorem C and the conflict rule (12.4, 9.4). Corollary W covers the
    confidentiality and integrity of frames toward the holders of a segment key, not who wrote a key entry or a recipient entry.
    That a recipient key identifier names one public key rests on collision resistance of `H_ctx`.
17. A NUMERIC SECURITY LEVEL IN THE STANDARD MODEL. The numbers of 13.4 are those of an ideal-cipher theorem, and the terms of the
    key schedule and of HMAC have no number from a theorem (`S-1` to `S-3`).
18. A QUANTUM ADVERSARY (13.6). Theorem C's level is the classical birthday bound of SHA-256; no quantum figure is stated.
19. A READER'S RESULT. Corollary F's confidentiality holds only toward a party that sees, of a reader's result, nothing but the
    outcomes of steps 1 to 7 of 7.2 for each sealed frame and the findings of clear frames that consult no opened content.
    Everything else a reader reports and delivers after it opens a frame may be a function of the plaintext and reveal it to
    whoever sees it. Examples, and the list is not complete: the outcome of step 8 (a malformed inner record or padding); of step
    9 (`damaged_frame`, or `exceeds_capacity` with `decoded_size` or `total_decoded`, which depend on the inner decoded length);
    of step 10 (every
    finding of the fold about the inner frame); the findings at other frames whose rules consult opened content, such as
    `seal_scope_violated`, `index_claim_failed` with `sealed_members`, and every finding of a clear frame that resolves against
    an inner frame or a sealed unit (terms, bindings and suppressions defined in an inner frame, recipes and values resolved
    against units of sealed packs); and the fold itself. Remedy: show a reader's result and content only to parties entitled to
    the plaintext.
20. A READER WHOSE KEY COMES FROM A RECIPIENT ENTRY. Corollary F is proved only for a reader that holds the segment key directly
    and holds no recipient key for an entry whose target is a key entry of the writer. For a reader that obtains it by unwrapping
    a recipient entry, or that holds such a recipient key beside it, none of Corollary F's claims about the file is made (question
    `Q-9`), and item 4 shows that such a reader can be given an adversary's key. Remedy: an authenticating signature over the
    `keys` frame and the sealed frames.

### 16.5 Mutations of that frame

- byte `0` of `sv` changed: the nonce changes: `seal_failed` with `aead`; one failed opening counted

  opening counted
- `ordinal` written `2`, identifiers recomputed: `malformed_value` with `sealed_ordinal`, for every reader, with no key
- the frame moved to ordinal `2` with `ordinal` rewritten to `2`, identifiers recomputed: `seal_failed` with `aead`
- one byte of `C` changed, payload digest not recomputed: `damaged_frame` with `payload_digest` at step 1; not opened, nothing
  counted
- one byte of `C` changed, payload digest and identifiers recomputed: `seal_failed` with `aead`

## 18. The construction beside published committing schemes

For the reviewer's orientation; no requirement rests on this section. Each line says what the cited text states and why its scheme
is not this one.

- CTX (Chan and Rogaway, "On Committing Authenticated-Encryption", ESORICS 2022) replaces an AEAD's tag `T` by `H(K, N, A, T)` and
  commits from collision resistance of `H`. It needs a decryption that recovers the message without the tag; AES-GCM-SIV has none,
  since its initial counter block is the tag with the most significant bit of the last byte set to one (RFC 8452, section 4).
- `CAU-SIV-C1` and `CAU-SIV-C4` (Bellare and Hoang, EUROCRYPT 2022) modify the tag computation of AES-GCM-SIV, so no unmodified
  library provides them; their commitment is about 64 bits in the ideal-cipher model at a 128-bit block.
- RtC and HtE (the same paper): RtC over an unmodified misuse-resistant scheme is one block longer and commits to the key only, at
  about 64 bits for AES; HtE adds no length and lifts a key-committing scheme to full commitment. `HtE[RtC[AES-256-GCM-SIV]]`
  commits fully with an unmodified cipher, one block longer, at about 64 bits, and takes a nonce as input.
- CEP (Grubbs, Lu and Ristenpart, "Message Franking via Committing Authenticated Encryption", CRYPTO 2017) commits by a
  collision-resistant pseudo-random function over (header, message), stored and recomputed on decryption, as this construction
  does; it takes a nonce, is stated for nonce-respecting adversaries, uses its own stream construction and stores two outputs.

This construction: deterministic, no nonce input, an unmodified cipher, 32 stored bytes that are both the nonce source and the
commitment, and full commitment of `(SK, AD, P)` at the collision level of SHA-256 from collision resistance alone. All of these
sit at the birthday bound of what they store; the difference is that this one needs no nonce and no modified cipher. Among the
texts cited no construction dominates it.

## 19. Assumptions, and questions for the reviewer

### 19.1 The closed list of assumptions

Theorems C and E, Lemmas H and K and Corollaries W and F are proved in sections 12 to 14 from the assumptions below and from
nothing else; each result names the assumptions it uses. The three assumptions about the container, `A-1` to `A-3`, are stated in
section 1 and are used by Corollary F, for a reader that holds the writer's segment key directly and holds no recipient key for an
entry whose target is a key entry of the writer (`A-1` and `A-3`; `A-2` is cited there only to record that nothing outside the
inputs of `Open` matters) and by section 10 (`A-3`). The assumptions about primitives are these, each with what the cited texts
establish.

**`S-0` Collision resistance of SHA-256.** Used by Theorem C, by explicit reduction (12.1), and by Lemma K (labels). FIPS 180-4
defines the function; no collision is known, and the generic classical cost is `2^128`. No quantum figure is assumed.

**`S-1` The key schedule.** From a uniform 32-byte `SK`, `(K_aead, K_commit)` of section 5 is indistinguishable from two uniform
independent keys (`eps_ks` of Theorem E). Two steps. EXTRACT: `PRK = HMAC(0^32, SK)` is two evaluations of the SHA-256 compression
function after the fixed first blocks, and in each the uniform (then pseudo-random) 32-byte value enters through the MESSAGE input
of the compression function under a fixed public chaining value. That it gives a pseudo-random output is the dual-PRF property of
the SHA-256 compression function, keyed through its message input, for one query each time: the same kind of assumption that
Bellare's proof for HMAC (`S-2`) makes of the compression function. Krawczyk ("Cryptographic Extraction and Key Derivation: The
HKDF Scheme", CRYPTO 2010, Lemma 5) states, in the random-oracle model for the compression function, an extraction result for a
fixed HMAC key and a source independent of that function; no standard-model statement for this step is cited, so it is this
assumption. EXPAND: HMAC-SHA-256 as a pseudo-random function keyed by `PRK` on the two distinct inputs of section 5; this is
assumption `S-2` for one key and two queries. Dropping the extract step (RFC 5869, section 3.3) would make `SK` itself the 32-byte
`PRK` and reduce `S-1` to `S-2` (question `Q-6`).

**`S-2` HMAC-SHA-256 is a pseudo-random function** under a uniform 32-byte key. Bellare ("New Proofs for NMAC and HMAC: Security
without Collision Resistance", Journal of Cryptology 28, 2015) proves it for HMAC over an iterated hash from properties of the
compression function, for a key of one full block; a 32-byte key padded with zero bytes, the use here, is outside the letter of
that theorem. For many keys the only statement cited is the hybrid: the multi-user advantage is at most `u` times the single-user
one, by the generic hybrid argument (remarked for authenticated encryption by Bellare and Tackmann, CRYPTO 2016, in their
introduction). Used by Theorem E and Lemma K.

**`S-3` AES-256-GCM-SIV is a multi-user misuse-resistant authenticated encryption scheme, with AES-256 an ideal cipher**, with the
numbers of 13.3. The statement used is Theorem 6 of Bose, Hoang and Tessaro (EUROCRYPT 2018, full version), in the ideal-cipher
model, with the adversary's ideal-cipher queries `p` as a parameter; the games of 11.3 and Theorem E are stated in that model for
this reason. The proof of that theorem is in its Appendix M, from results of the same paper; this part rests on the statement. The
designers' single-key bound (Gueron, Langley and Lindell, "AES-GCM-SIV: Specification and Analysis", Cryptology ePrint Archive,
Report 2017/168; no revision of it was read for this part) is not used here;
Iwata and Seurin ("Reconsidering the Security Bound of AES-GCM-SIV", IACR Transactions on Symmetric Cryptology 2017(4), pages 240
to 267; Cryptology ePrint Archive, Report 2017/708) give a corrected single-key bound. No bound cited is in the standard model for
many keys. The reduction of Theorem E also uses that AES-256-GCM-SIV is tidy, which is a fact of its specification (section 2),
not an assumption.

**`S-4` X-Wing is an IND-CCA key encapsulation mechanism in the random-oracle model** for SHA3-256, SHA3-512 and SHAKE-256, from
the IND-CCA security of ML-KEM-768 and the gap computational Diffie-Hellman problem on `Curve25519`, as
`draft-connolly-cfrg-xwing-kem-11` states it (its section 6, citing its proof reference). That draft also states binding
properties of X-Wing, which it names `MAL-BIND-K-PK` and `MAL-BIND-K-CT`, with the reference to a proof still to be supplied (its
section 6.1), and states that they do not hold when the regular ML-KEM decapsulation key is transmitted between implementations
(its section 5.5.1); hence the seed form of `P8-52`. They are not part of this assumption, and no proof here uses them. The
defining text is an individual Internet-Draft. Used by Lemma K.

**`S-5` AES-256-GCM is a one-time authenticated encryption scheme under many independent keys** (the game `AE1` of 11.5), with
AES-256 the ideal cipher of 11.3 when it is used with Theorem E. Each key of the wrap encrypts exactly one 32-byte message under
one nonce. NIST SP 800-38D defines the mode. Used by Lemma K.

**Remark `R-1` (not an assumption of the theorems).** The keyed and key-derivation modes of BLAKE3 are not used in a keyed role.
The BLAKE3 paper (O'Connor, Aumasson, Neves and Wilcox-O'Hearn, "BLAKE3: one function, fast everywhere", 2020) claims 128-bit
collision resistance for the hash and gives an indifferentiability bound for its mode over an idealised compression function; it
states neither property for the keyed mode with the key as an input, so using it for `sv` would add an assumption that no
published text states. Collision resistance of `H_ctx` is an assumption of the container, and this part relies on it only in
Corollary F (that equal header identifiers mean equal headers) and in items 13 and 16 of section 15.

### 19.2 Questions for the reviewer, most important first

**`Q-0` What a reader gets (14.3).** Is Corollary F the right statement of what a reader of a file gets, and is its proof (each
opening a decryption query; the associated data a function of the file by `A-1` and `A-3`; storage and signatures outside the seal
by `A-2`) complete? Is the restriction to a reader that holds the writer's key directly and holds no recipient key for an entry
whose target is a key entry of the writer, and to outcomes of steps 1 to 7 for confidentiality, the right boundary?

**`Q-1` The reduction of Theorem E (13.2).** Is the ideal-cipher framing of the game `DAE` (11.3) acceptable? Is `B'` an adversary
of the multi-user game of Bose, Hoang and Tessaro as used: at most two encryption queries per (user, nonce), stopped at `Coll3`;
verification queries only where `B`'s table holds no matching entry; primitive queries forwarded? Is the case analysis for a
decryption query that the cipher accepts with no table entry, which uses tidiness, complete? Is the ideal world of 11.3, with two
failure symbols, the right notion for a reader that reports both?

**`Q-2` Theorem C and Lemma H (12).** Is the case analysis complete, and is the prefix-freeness of `𝒜` (6.2, 11.1) a sufficient
basis for the injectivity of `AD || P`?

**`Q-3` The wrap (9, 14.1, 14.2).** Is the KEM/DEM composition of Lemma K sound for HPKE base mode with this suite: `S-4` for the
KEM, HMAC as a pseudo-random function for `secret` and for the expansion, the label entering through `info_hash`, and one-time
security of AES-256-GCM? Is the one hop of Corollary W sound with the unwrap oracle as defined? HPKE's key schedule does not take
`enc`; this part binds the context through `info` and leaves `enc` to the KEM. Should `enc` also be bound (for instance through
`aad`)? Is the stop event `Coll_enc` of Lemma K (repeated encapsulations to one recipient, and an encapsulation named by an unwrap
query before it is returned) bounded correctly by `K_0` from `S-4` and `δ`, and does the one-challenge game of `S-4` allow the
challenge to be requested at a time that the adversary chooses, as `K_0` and `K_1` need? Theorem E does not rest on one
reading of the verification restriction of the game of Bose, Hoang and Tessaro (Figure 1): its reduction stops before a query
that the order-free reading forbids, and the bound carries the term `q_e * q_d / 2^136` (13.2, THE STOP OF `B`). Is that stop
sound, and is the term needed at all?

**`Q-4` Dishonest recipient keys.** What, if anything, is lost for a recipient whose key is not honestly generated or not in seed
form, given that a reader that obtains two different keys for one key entry opens nothing (9.4) and that Theorem C prevents two
keys from opening one frame?

**`Q-5` The numbers (13.3 to 13.5).** Is the instantiation of Theorem 6 of Bose, Hoang and Tessaro correct: `ε = 1/8`, `a = 12`,
`c = 1.5`, `B` counting both encryption queries of a (user, nonce) and the associated-data blocks, `L` counting verification
queries? Are the limits `2^32` frames of `2^27` bytes per key and `2^10` failed openings per read appropriate, given that the term
over many keys grows with the total volume and that the bound is meaningful only up to about `2^42` keys at the full limit?

**`Q-6` The keyed hash and the schedule (2, 5).** Is HMAC-SHA-256 for `sv` the right choice beside BLAKE3 elsewhere, or should
every keyed role use the keyed modes of BLAKE3 under an assumption stated for them? Is the extract step with the fixed salt `0^32`
worth keeping for a uniform 32-byte `SK`, or should the schedule be the expand step alone (RFC 5869, section 3.3), which would
replace `S-1` by `S-2`?

**`Q-7` What the associated data binds (6.2).** Is binding the ordinal and not the predecessor the right trade, given that a frame
then survives damage to its predecessor's identifier but can be replaced by a frame of an earlier rendering at the same place (15
item 3)?

**`Q-8` Leakage (10.2).** Is the list of what a keyless party learns complete, in particular for the compression channel under one
padding policy and for the key table?

**`Q-9` A reader whose key comes from a recipient entry (14.3, 15 item 20).** Does Corollary F extend to a reader that obtains the
writer's key by unwrapping a recipient entry? A proof would answer unwraps through the unwrap oracle of `DAE-W`, treat an
unwrapped key as the writer's only for the game's own entries, and pay the probability that an entry the adversary wrote unwraps
to a writer's key, which decides the `key_conflict` outcome the simulator cannot otherwise compute. What are the extra terms (one
expects `Adv_lcca`, about `q_u / 2^256`, and `n_w * δ` for honest entries that do not decapsulate), and is anything else lost?
